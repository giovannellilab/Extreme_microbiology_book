# FIGURE_STYLE.md

## Purpose

Figures in *Microbiology of Extreme Environments* must form a coherent visual family across the book.

The house style is:

NATURAL HISTORY MUSEUM MINIMAL

The goal is to combine:
- scientific clarity;
- technical accuracy;
- visual warmth;
- restrained natural-history character;
- contemporary readability;
- strong consistency across chapters.

Figures should feel like they belong to the same scientific museum exhibition or modern natural-history atlas.

They must NOT look like:
- generic modern textbook infographics;
- glossy biotech/startup graphics;
- cold engineering drawings;
- comic-book or science-fiction art;
- highly ornate historical natural-history plates;
- decorative illustrations with little explanatory value.

The visual style serves the science. It must never obscure it.

---

## 1. Core visual language

Use:
- generous negative space;
- clear visual hierarchy;
- restrained detail;
- fine, precise linework;
- muted, natural colours;
- limited accent colours;
- soft visual texture only when it contributes to the natural-history feel;
- clean and controlled annotation.

Use a pure white background as the default figure field. Light tonal fills may be used locally within panels, insets or figure elements when they improve separation, but the overall page/background field of the figure should remain white.

Avoid:
- clutter;
- ornamental borders;
- excessive texture;
- glossy 3D effects;
- strong artificial gradients;
- unnecessary shadows;
- saturated rainbow palettes;
- dense icon systems;
- decorative elements that do not communicate scientific information.

The desired balance is:

warm but technical;
illustrative but precise;
distinctive but not stylised for its own sake;
minimal but not sterile.

---

## 2. Typography

All text inside figures, including titles and subtitles, must use a clean sans serif typeface.

Figure titles should normally be included inside the figure itself, typically at the top-left, following the visual model of the approved style tests. A short subtitle may appear directly below the title where useful.

Use sans serif for:
- figure titles;
- panel labels;
- axis labels;
- annotations;
- callouts;
- legends;
- labels;
- scale-bar text;
- inset headings;
- table text contained within figures.

Do not use serif fonts inside figures.

Typography should be:
- simple;
- highly legible;
- visually quiet;
- consistent between figures.

Use weight and size rather than decorative typography to establish hierarchy.

Recommended hierarchy:
- panel letters: bold;
- short panel headings: semibold/bold;
- scientific annotations: regular;
- secondary explanatory text: regular, smaller;
- taxonomic names: italic according to book conventions.

For final book placement, assume a maximum final figure width of approximately 180 mm. No text may fall below 8.5 pt at final placed size. Preferred label text is 9–11 pt at final size; secondary labels may be 8.5–9 pt; panel letters should generally be 12–14 pt; and short in-figure headings should generally be 11–14 pt. If readability would require smaller text, simplify the figure rather than shrinking the typography.

Avoid ALL CAPS except where a very short structural label genuinely benefits from it.

---

## 3. Colour

Colour must encode scientific or structural meaning.

Use a restrained palette based on:
- muted blues;
- teal;
- desaturated greens;
- ochre;
- warm orange;
- rust;
- muted red;
- purple where scientifically useful;
- neutral greys;
- cream/off-white local fills.

Do not assign a fixed colour to every concept in the book unless the same meaning recurs consistently.

Use colour to:
- distinguish states;
- separate domains or compartments;
- indicate gradients;
- highlight the focal mechanism;
- distinguish environmental zones;
- identify related elements.

Do not use colour merely to make a figure more attractive.

Figures must remain interpretable when:
- printed;
- viewed on small screens;
- viewed by readers with common colour-vision deficiencies.

Do not rely on colour alone. Combine colour with:
- position;
- labels;
- line style;
- shape;
- symbols;
- pattern where necessary.

---

## 4. Linework and illustration character

Scientific drawings should use fine, controlled linework with slightly organic character.

The illustration should feel hand-crafted enough to avoid generic vector-art sterility, but not visibly sketchy or informal.

Prefer crisp scientific illustration over painterly rendering. Use restrained tonal modelling, clear edges, sparse texture and generous negative space. Avoid soft, airbrushed, glossy or overly rendered surfaces.

Avoid:
- cartoon outlines;
- exaggerated contours;
- decorative hatching everywhere;
- hyperrealistic cellular rendering;
- excessive microscopic detail that is irrelevant to the teaching point.

Cellular and molecular structures may be simplified.

Any simplification must preserve the scientific feature being taught.

When a drawing is schematic rather than to scale, make this clear where misunderstanding is possible.

---

## 5. Four principal figure types

The house style must work across four major figure classes.

### A. Mechanism / cellular-process figures

Examples:
- thermophile membrane adaptation;
- cold-active enzymes;
- osmoadaptation;
- proton motive force;
- DNA protection;
- carbon fixation pathways.

Requirements:
- one dominant scientific question per figure;
- causal sequence should be visually obvious;
- use callouts and insets sparingly;
- do not turn the figure into a molecular inventory;
- distinguish the central mechanism from supporting detail;
- show only structures needed to understand the mechanism.

Whenever possible, organise the figure around:

environmental condition
→ molecular/cellular consequence
→ biological problem
→ adaptation
→ functional outcome.

---

### B. Environmental / habitat figures

Examples:
- hydrothermal vents;
- hot springs;
- sea ice;
- hypersaline lakes;
- acidic drainage;
- deep subsurface fractures.

These may be more visually atmospheric than mechanism figures, but must remain scientifically controlled.

Show:
- major physical structure;
- environmental gradients;
- relevant chemical or energetic zones;
- microbial habitats or processes;
- scale where useful.

Environmental scenes should not become fantasy landscapes.

If a real photograph exists and is scientifically useful, prefer the photograph or use it alongside a schematic.

Original field photographs by the authors should be treated as first-class scientific material.

---

### C. Structural / comparative figures

Examples:
- prokaryote versus eukaryote architecture;
- bacterial versus archaeal membranes;
- alternative osmoadaptation strategies;
- different hydrothermal vent architectures.

Requirements:
- use visual symmetry where it improves comparison;
- compare the same conceptual dimensions across panels;
- avoid irrelevant anatomical detail;
- direct labels are preferred over large legends;
- scale must be explicit where relevant.

The comparison should answer a clear question rather than simply place two objects side by side.

---

### D. Abstract / quantitative conceptual diagrams

Examples:
- cardinal temperature curves;
- salinity growth ranges;
- pH ranges;
- pressure–temperature niches;
- redox-energy diagrams;
- simplified phase-space diagrams.

Requirements:
- visual simplicity;
- precise axes;
- clear units;
- minimal decoration;
- restrained use of colour;
- readable at small size.

These figures should still belong visually to the same museum-minimal family through typography, palette, spacing and annotation.

Do not force illustrative texture into figures where it reduces quantitative clarity.

Do not add decorative microorganisms, random morphologies or unlabeled biological forms to abstract diagrams. Include organism drawings only when they represent a scientifically specific example, are clearly identified, and materially improve understanding.

---

## 6. Photographs

Photographs are distinct from illustrations and should remain visually authentic.

Do not artificially stylise scientific photographs to resemble drawings.

Prefer:
1. original author photographs;
2. properly licensed scientific photographs;
3. external images only when they add clear scientific value.

When annotating photographs, use the same house system:
- sans serif labels;
- thin clean leader lines;
- restrained annotation boxes;
- consistent panel lettering;
- scale bars where relevant.

Photographs can be combined with diagrams where this improves interpretation.

Example:
author photograph of a black smoker
+
minimal labels or an adjacent conceptual gradient diagram.

---

## 7. Redrawing established scientific concepts

Scientific concepts may be redrawn in the house style when useful.

Do NOT trace or cosmetically modify copyrighted textbook artwork.

Instead:
- identify the underlying scientific concept;
- verify it from appropriate scientific sources;
- redesign the composition;
- change the explanatory structure where useful;
- use the book's own visual hierarchy and annotations.

A redrawn figure should improve, simplify or reframe the explanation.

It should not exist merely to imitate a familiar published figure with different colours.

Canonical scientific geometries may be retained where the geometry itself represents the scientific idea, but the artwork and composition must be original.

---

## 8. Labels and annotations

Prefer direct labelling over legends whenever practical.

Labels should:
- be concise;
- sit close to the element they describe;
- avoid crossing leader lines;
- avoid covering important structures;
- use consistent spacing and alignment.

Callout boxes should be used sparingly.

Do not place long textbook paragraphs inside figures.

The figure should communicate visually; the caption provides depth.

---

## 9. Scale and scientific representation

Use scale bars whenever actual physical scale matters.

If elements are not shown to scale, state this where the reader could reasonably assume otherwise.

Do not imply quantitative precision through illustration when none exists.

Gradients, environmental zones and distributions should distinguish clearly between:
- measured values;
- representative ranges;
- conceptual relationships.

---

## 10. Panels

Multi-panel figures are acceptable when the panels answer a single coherent question.

Use:
A, B, C, D...

Panel lettering:
- sans serif;
- bold;
- prominent but not decorative.

Do not create multi-panel figures merely to compress unrelated content.

Each panel should contribute to the same teaching objective.

---

## 11. Captions

Captions must explain:
- what the figure shows;
- the main scientific point;
- relevant simplifications;
- important evidence boundaries;
- source/data provenance where applicable.

Captions should not simply repeat labels.

For conceptual figures:
state that the figure is schematic where appropriate.

For literature-derived quantitative figures:
cite the relevant sources.

For redrawn conceptual material:
cite scientific sources supporting the concept, not the visual design.

For photographs:
credit the photographer and licence where applicable.

---

## 12. Tables

Tables should follow the same visual identity:
- sans serif typography;
- minimal rules;
- generous spacing;
- restrained use of background shading;
- no decorative borders;
- clear hierarchy.

Use tables for comparison or compact factual structure, not as substitutes for explanatory prose.

---

## 13. Accessibility and production

Every figure must:
- remain legible on screen and in print;
- work at the expected publication size;
- contain adequate contrast;
- not rely solely on colour;
- include alt text;
- have a complete caption.

Preferred formats:
- SVG for vector diagrams;
- PDF where required for print/vector preservation;
- high-resolution PNG/TIFF only for raster material or photographs.

Where possible, retain an editable source.

When necessary, figures may be produced as a hybrid workflow: image-generated base artwork plus vector/text overlays for labels, axes, panel letters or precise annotations. Use this approach when it improves typographic crispness, annotation consistency or quantitative clarity.

“Figure complete” means that the scientific concept is approved; one final asset is selected; rejected or intermediate candidates are removed from publication directories or clearly excluded from publication; a final production filename is assigned; the caption, alt text, prose callout and provenance/licensing are complete; the chapter references the final asset; and the rendered result has been inspected successfully. Generating candidates alone does not satisfy this definition.

Publication directories must not contain ambiguous competing versions without an explicit production reason. Retained editable bases, overlays or other production sources must be named clearly as source assets.

---

## 14. Scientific accuracy

Figure aesthetics never override scientific accuracy.

Before finalising a figure, verify:
- terminology;
- organism names;
- taxonomy where relevant;
- units;
- ranges;
- environmental conditions;
- molecular structures;
- process direction;
- causal interpretation.

Do not introduce unsupported mechanisms simply because they make a visually satisfying diagram.

---

## 15. What to avoid

Reject figures that drift toward:

### Generic textbook infographic
- bright flat icons;
- excessive coloured boxes;
- infographic-like box-heavy layouts;
- modular dashboard layouts;
- visual language resembling educational software.
- decorative biology added only for visual interest;
- generic cell cartoons disconnected from the scientific message.

### Ornate natural-history plate
- heavy decorative borders;
- excessive biological detail;
- dense hatching;
- visual complexity that competes with the explanation.

### Comic / science-fiction illustration
- exaggerated perspective;
- dramatic cinematic lighting;
- narrative characters;
- stylisation that dominates the scientific content.

### Cold technical drafting
- monochrome engineering aesthetic;
- excessively rigid geometry;
- visual austerity that removes biological character.

The target lies between these extremes.

---

## 16. Final visual test

Before accepting a figure, ask:

1. What scientific question does this figure answer?
2. Is that answer visually obvious?
3. Does every major element contribute to that answer?
4. Is anything present only for decoration?
5. Could some prose be removed because the figure now explains it better?
6. Is the figure scientifically defensible?
7. Does it remain understandable without relying on colour?
8. Does it look recognisably part of *Microbiology of Extreme Environments*?
9. Is it warm and distinctive without becoming artistic for its own sake?
10. Would it still work in print five or ten years from now?

If not, revise it.
