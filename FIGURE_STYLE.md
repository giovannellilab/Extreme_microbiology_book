# FIGURE_STYLE.md

## Purpose

Figures in *Microbiology of Extreme Environments* must form a coherent visual family across the book.

House style:

**Natural History Museum Minimal**

The visual standard combines:

- scientific precision;
- technical accuracy;
- restrained natural-history character;
- visual warmth;
- contemporary readability;
- strong consistency across chapters.

Figures should feel as though they belong to the same modern scientific museum exhibition or natural-history atlas.

They must not look like:

- generic textbook infographics;
- presentation slides;
- educational software;
- glossy biotech or startup graphics;
- cold engineering diagrams;
- cartoons;
- science-fiction scenes;
- ornate historical plates;
- decorative illustrations with little explanatory value.

The visual style serves the science.

Technical validity, readable labels and successful rendering are necessary but not sufficient.

# 1. Approved quality references

Use successful book figures as visual-quality references.

Current principal references include:

- `book/figures/fig19_02_cellular_adaptations.png`
- `book/figures/fig19_03_thermal_geochemical_gradient.png`
- `book/figures/fig19_01_cardinal_temperatures.svg`

These references serve different purposes.

## Illustrated cellular reference

`fig19_02_cellular_adaptations.png` demonstrates:

- a scientifically controlled illustrated object;
- restrained but sufficient biological detail;
- an image-generated base combined with precise labels and callouts;
- visual warmth without decorative excess;
- a clear central object;
- crisp typographic hierarchy;
- effective use of insets.

## Illustrated environmental reference

`fig19_03_thermal_geochemical_gradient.png` demonstrates:

- an environmental scene as the dominant visual object;
- integration of physical setting, gradients and microbial niches;
- image-generated or illustrative environmental artwork;
- restrained text overlays;
- direct labels;
- a figure that explains spatial relationships more effectively than boxes and arrows.

## Abstract vector reference

`fig19_01_cardinal_temperatures.svg` demonstrates where pure vector production is appropriate:

- an inherently abstract or quantitative relationship;
- precise geometry;
- limited visual elements;
- no need for an illustrated natural object or habitat.

Reference figures establish quality, hierarchy and production logic.

Do not copy their scientific content, composition or palette mechanically.

New figures must remain original.

# 2. Scientific purpose first

Every figure must answer one clear scientific or pedagogical question.

Begin with the scientific object or relationship:

- cell;
- organism;
- molecule;
- membrane;
- habitat;
- interface;
- gradient;
- dataset;
- comparison;
- process.

Do not begin with a layout of boxes, panels or arrows.

Before production, state:

- the scientific question;
- what the reader must be able to see;
- which details are essential;
- which details must be omitted;
- whether the relationship is measured, representative, conceptual or schematic.

A major figure should visualise a major concept.

It must not document the evidence-audit process or reproduce the chapter outline.

# 3. Production mode must be fixed before generation

Every major figure must have an approved production mode in `GATE1_PLAN.md`.

Allowed modes are:

- scientific illustration;
- photograph with restrained annotation;
- image-generated scientific illustration with vector/text overlay;
- quantitative plot;
- simple vector schematic;
- hybrid composition.

Do not change production mode merely because another method is faster or easier.

If the approved mode proves unsuitable, document the problem and return the proposed change to the editor.

File format and production mode are not the same.

An SVG may contain a raster illustration and vector overlay.

A PNG may contain a highly diagrammatic composition.

Choose the method from the scientific object, not the file extension.

# 4. Production-mode decision

Use the following decision logic.

## Use scientific illustration or a hybrid when the figure shows:

- a cell;
- a microorganism;
- a habitat;
- a natural environmental scene;
- an organism–environment interaction;
- anatomical or ultrastructural relationships;
- a spatially complex natural interface;
- a process whose physical setting is essential to understanding it.

## Use a photograph with annotation when:

- seeing the real environment or specimen materially improves understanding;
- an authentic field image is available;
- scale, texture or morphology matters;
- illustration would unnecessarily abstract a recognisable scientific object.

## Use a quantitative plot when:

- the scientific point depends on measured values;
- axes, units, distributions or uncertainty are central;
- visual precision is more important than illustrative character.

## Use a simple vector schematic only when:

- the subject is inherently abstract;
- precise geometry or sequence is the main information;
- an illustrated physical object would not improve understanding;
- the composition can remain visually simple;
- the editor has not approved a different production mode.

Do not use flat vector graphics for environmental scenes merely because they are easy to produce.

Do not use a generic diagram when the subject is a natural object that should be seen.

# 5. Core visual language

Use:

- a pure white overall figure field;
- generous negative space;
- a clear dominant visual object;
- restrained detail;
- fine, precise linework;
- slightly organic but controlled forms;
- muted natural colours;
- limited accent colours;
- clean annotation;
- direct labelling;
- consistent spacing and hierarchy.

Local tonal fields may be used where they clarify:

- insets;
- environmental zones;
- compartments;
- gradients;
- comparisons.

The desired balance is:

- warm but technical;
- illustrative but precise;
- distinctive but not theatrical;
- minimal but not sterile;
- naturalistic but not photorealistic.

Avoid:

- dashboard layouts;
- modular cards;
- rounded interface-style boxes;
- repeated icon systems;
- decorative borders;
- excessive arrows;
- excessive texture;
- glossy surfaces;
- strong artificial gradients;
- unnecessary shadows;
- saturated rainbow palettes;
- decorative microorganisms;
- visual elements added only to fill empty space.

Muted colours alone do not make a generic infographic consistent with the house style.

# 6. Typography

All text inside figures must use a clean sans serif typeface.

Figure titles should normally appear inside the figure at the top-left.

A short subtitle may appear directly below the title when useful.

Use sans serif for:

- titles;
- subtitles;
- panel labels;
- axis labels;
- annotations;
- callouts;
- legends;
- scale-bar text;
- inset headings.

Use weight and size rather than decorative typography.

At final publication size:

- preferred labels: 9–11 pt;
- secondary labels: 8.5–9 pt;
- panel letters: 12–14 pt;
- short headings: 11–14 pt;
- nothing below 8.5 pt.

Assume a maximum final width of approximately 180 mm.

If the typography would need to be smaller, simplify the figure.

Scientific names follow the book’s italicisation rules.

Avoid all caps except for very short structural labels where it clearly improves hierarchy.

Do not allow an image-generation model to produce final text.

Add all final titles, labels, axes and annotations as controlled overlays.

# 7. Colour

Colour must communicate scientific or structural meaning.

Use a restrained palette based on:

- muted blues;
- teal;
- desaturated greens;
- ochre;
- warm orange;
- rust;
- muted red;
- purple where scientifically useful;
- neutral greys;
- cream or off-white local fills.

Use colour to show:

- states;
- compartments;
- environmental zones;
- gradients;
- related elements;
- focal mechanisms.

Do not use colour merely to make a composition more attractive.

Do not rely on colour alone.

Combine colour with:

- position;
- labels;
- line style;
- shape;
- pattern where necessary.

Figures must remain interpretable:

- in print;
- on small screens;
- with common colour-vision deficiencies.

# 8. Illustration character

Scientific illustration should use:

- clear edges;
- restrained tonal modelling;
- sparse texture;
- slightly organic linework;
- controlled simplification;
- visually coherent depth.

The illustration should feel crafted rather than assembled from generic vector primitives.

Prefer crisp scientific illustration over:

- painterly rendering;
- soft airbrushing;
- glossy 3D rendering;
- clip-art shapes;
- exaggerated outlines;
- unnecessary microscopic detail.

Cellular, molecular and environmental features may be simplified.

Any simplification must preserve the feature being taught.

When an illustration is schematic or not to scale, state this where misunderstanding is possible.

# 9. Mechanism and cellular figures

Show one dominant scientific question.

Use only structures needed to explain the mechanism.

Where useful, organise around:

environmental condition  
→ cellular consequence  
→ biological problem  
→ adaptation  
→ functional outcome

Do not turn the figure into a molecular inventory.

A cellular figure should normally contain a recognisable scientific object rather than a collection of abstract icons.

Insets should enlarge or clarify specific mechanisms.

They should not function as unrelated mini-figures.

# 10. Environmental and habitat figures

Environmental figures must make the physical environment visually intelligible.

The environmental object should dominate the composition.

Show only the relevant combination of:

- physical structure;
- fluid or material movement;
- gradients;
- environmental chemistry;
- energy sources;
- microbial habitats;
- microbial processes;
- representative organisms;
- spatial or temporal change.

Environmental scenes may be more atmospheric than quantitative diagrams, but they must remain scientifically controlled.

They must not become fantasy landscapes.

Environmental figures should normally use:

- scientific illustration;
- authentic photography;
- an image-generated scientific illustration with controlled overlays;
- a hybrid composition.

A pure vector schematic is acceptable only when the environment is intentionally abstracted and the editor has approved that abstraction.

Reject an environmental figure when:

- the habitat is reduced to boxes and arrows;
- natural structures are represented by generic geometric icons;
- text occupies more visual attention than the environment;
- the figure resembles a presentation slide;
- the illustration contains arbitrary organisms or geological features;
- the physical scene could be replaced by any unrelated environment without changing the design.

Original author photographs should be treated as first-class scientific material.

# 11. Quantitative and abstract figures

Use vector production for:

- plots;
- axes;
- curves;
- phase spaces;
- measured comparisons;
- abstract relationships;
- simple process sequences.

Requirements include:

- precise axes;
- explicit units;
- clear uncertainty where relevant;
- minimal decoration;
- restrained colour;
- readable annotations;
- accurate geometry.

Do not force illustrative texture into a quantitative plot.

Do not add decorative organisms, cells or habitat silhouettes.

Abstract figures must still belong to the visual family through:

- typography;
- spacing;
- palette;
- annotation;
- line weight;
- hierarchy.

# 12. Comparative figures

Compare the same conceptual dimensions across panels or objects.

Use symmetry when it improves comparison.

Make the comparison question explicit.

Avoid irrelevant anatomical or environmental detail.

Do not place two objects side by side without identifying what the reader should compare.

Use a consistent scale or explicitly state when scale differs.

# 13. Photographs

Photographs should remain visually authentic.

Prefer:

1. original author photographs;
2. appropriately licensed scientific photographs;
3. external photographs only when they add clear scientific value.

Do not stylise photographs to resemble drawings.

Use:

- restrained labels;
- thin leader lines;
- consistent typography;
- scale bars where relevant;
- minimal local shading only when necessary for label contrast.

Photographs may be combined with:

- conceptual overlays;
- adjacent plots;
- magnified scientific illustrations;
- simplified spatial annotations.

Do not obscure the scientific content of the photograph with excessive annotation.

# 14. Image-generated and hybrid production

Image generation is appropriate for:

- scientific natural-history illustration;
- cells;
- organisms;
- habitats;
- environmental scenes;
- scientifically controlled cutaways;
- spatial relationships difficult to photograph.

Use image generation to create the visual scientific object—not the final typographic layer.

The generated base should normally omit:

- titles;
- labels;
- legends;
- arrows;
- axes;
- long annotations;
- decorative borders.

Add these later as controlled vector or text overlays.

An image-generation prompt must specify:

- the scientific object;
- essential structures;
- prohibited structures;
- viewpoint;
- degree of simplification;
- white background or intended compositional field;
- restrained natural colours;
- crisp scientific-natural-history rendering;
- absence of generated text;
- absence of decorative organisms;
- absence of cinematic lighting;
- absence of fantasy features.

Generated images must be inspected for:

- invented anatomy;
- impossible geometry;
- duplicated structures;
- inconsistent scales;
- incorrect environmental relationships;
- decorative but misleading features;
- text-like artefacts;
- unrequested organisms.

Do not assume that a polished generated image is scientifically correct.

A hybrid workflow should normally proceed:

1. settle the scientific concept;
2. create the visual brief;
3. generate or select the base illustration;
4. inspect and correct the base;
5. add precise labels and annotations;
6. compare with approved quality references;
7. inspect at final publication size;
8. export the final asset.

# 15. Redrawing established scientific concepts

Scientific concepts may be redrawn in the house style.

Do not trace or cosmetically modify copyrighted artwork.

Instead:

- identify the underlying scientific concept;
- verify it from appropriate sources;
- design a new composition;
- change the explanatory structure where useful;
- apply the book’s visual hierarchy;
- create original artwork.

A redrawn figure should improve, simplify or reframe the explanation.

It should not imitate a familiar published figure with different colours.

# 16. Labels, annotations and panels

Prefer direct labels over legends.

Labels should:

- be concise;
- sit close to the element described;
- avoid crossing leader lines;
- avoid covering important structures;
- use consistent alignment;
- use consistent spacing.

Callout boxes should be used sparingly.

Do not place textbook paragraphs inside figures.

The figure should communicate visually; the caption provides depth.

Multi-panel figures are acceptable when all panels answer one coherent question.

Use:

A, B, C, D...

for panel labels.

Do not combine unrelated content merely to save space.

# 17. Scale and quantitative meaning

Use scale bars when actual physical scale matters.

If elements are not shown to scale, state this where a reader could reasonably assume otherwise.

Do not imply quantitative precision through illustration when none exists.

Distinguish clearly among:

- measured values;
- representative ranges;
- conceptual gradients;
- schematic relationships.

Do not place precise-looking numerical gradients over an illustration unless supported by a defined dataset.

# 18. Captions, alt text and provenance

Captions must explain:

- what the figure shows;
- the main scientific point;
- relevant simplifications;
- important evidence boundaries;
- source or data provenance;
- licensing where applicable.

Captions should not simply repeat labels.

For quantitative figures, cite the underlying data or literature.

For conceptual figures, cite the science supporting the concept where necessary.

For photographs, credit the photographer and licence.

For generated or hybrid figures, state that the composition is original and identify the production approach where appropriate.

Alt text should describe the scientific information conveyed by the figure.

It should not merely repeat the caption or list visual elements without explaining their relationship.

# 19. Production formats and resolution

Use:

- SVG or PDF for vector plots and diagrams;
- high-resolution PNG or TIFF for raster illustrations and photographs;
- SVG, PDF or high-resolution PNG for hybrid compositions, depending on the overlay workflow.

A full-width raster figure should normally be at least approximately 2400 pixels wide unless the production system establishes a different requirement.

Retain an editable source when useful.

Source assets must be clearly identified, for example:

`fig19_02_cellular_adaptations_source.svg`

`fig19_02_cellular_adaptations_source_base.png`

Publication assets must use stable final names.

Do not reference editable source files from the chapter.

# 20. Candidate management

Generating a candidate does not complete a figure.

For every major figure:

- inspect the actual output;
- reject scientifically or visually weak candidates;
- select one final version;
- remove rejected candidates from publication paths;
- retain production sources only with unambiguous names.

Do not keep multiple files that appear to be competing final versions.

Do not accept the first technically valid candidate by default.

# 21. Figure-quality review

Every major figure must receive a documented visual review before Gate 2.

The review must compare the figure with:

- `FIGURE_STYLE.md`;
- the approved Gate 1 production mode;
- the designated successful reference figures;
- its appearance at final placed size.

Assess:

- scientific purpose;
- scientific accuracy;
- visual hierarchy;
- production-mode compliance;
- illustration quality;
- label clarity;
- typography;
- accessibility;
- stylistic consistency;
- whether the figure resembles a generic infographic;
- whether it materially improves understanding.

Report:

Visual quality:

- Publication quality
- Strong
- Generic
- Unsatisfactory

Figure readiness:

- Ready
- Minor revision
- Major revision
- Reject and regenerate

A figure rated Generic or Unsatisfactory cannot proceed to Gate 2.

Successful rendering cannot override visual rejection.

# 22. Explicit rejection criteria

Reject figures that exhibit any of the following.

## Generic infographic

- flat geometric icons standing in for natural structures;
- modular cards or dashboard composition;
- rounded boxes around conceptual categories;
- repeated arrows between labelled blocks;
- excessive explanatory text inside the figure;
- a presentation-slide appearance;
- generic vector shapes with a muted palette;
- diagrammatic organisms disconnected from real morphology;
- an environmental scene reduced to symbols.

## Vector sterility

- rigid geometric construction where an organic scientific object is needed;
- no natural surface, morphology or environmental character;
- elements assembled from standard primitives;
- linework that resembles software UI graphics;
- a scene that communicates mainly through labels rather than visual structure.

## Decorative natural history

- organisms added only for visual interest;
- irrelevant anatomical detail;
- dense texture;
- ornamental borders;
- unnecessary hatching;
- a historical-plate appearance that competes with explanation.

## Cinematic or science-fiction art

- dramatic lighting;
- exaggerated perspective;
- narrative characters;
- fantasy colours;
- monumental or heroic composition;
- stylisation that overwhelms the science.

## Scientific illustration failure

- invented morphology;
- impossible spatial relationships;
- misleading scale;
- ambiguous process direction;
- inaccurate molecular structures;
- unsupported environmental features;
- visual precision unsupported by evidence.

# 23. Scientific accuracy

Aesthetics never override scientific accuracy.

Before accepting a figure, verify as relevant:

- terminology;
- organism names;
- taxonomy;
- units;
- ranges;
- environmental conditions;
- molecular structures;
- process direction;
- causal interpretation;
- scale;
- evidence status.

Do not introduce unsupported biology because it makes the figure cleaner or more attractive.

Do not retain an inaccurate element merely because it is visually successful.

# 24. Definition of figure complete

A figure is complete only when:

- the scientific question is settled;
- the production mode matches Gate 1;
- the visual brief is satisfied;
- one final asset is selected;
- scientific accuracy is checked;
- visual quality review has passed;
- typography is legible at placed size;
- the final filename is assigned;
- the chapter references the final asset;
- the prose calls it out;
- the caption is complete;
- alt text is complete;
- provenance and licensing are recorded;
- the rendered figure has been inspected.

Generating candidates alone does not satisfy this definition.

Technical correctness alone does not satisfy this definition.

# 25. Final visual test

Before accepting a figure, ask:

1. What scientific question does this figure answer?
2. Is the answer visually obvious?
3. Is the correct scientific object visually dominant?
4. Does every major element contribute?
5. Is anything present only for decoration?
6. Is the production mode the one approved at Gate 1?
7. Would another production mode communicate the science better?
8. Is the figure scientifically defensible?
9. Is it legible at final publication size?
10. Does it work without relying only on colour?
11. Does it resemble a generic infographic or presentation slide?
12. Does it meet the quality of the designated successful book references?
13. Does it look recognisably part of *Microbiology of Extreme Environments*?
14. Could prose be removed because the figure now explains the relationship effectively?
15. Would the figure remain useful and visually credible in print five or ten years from now?

If any central answer is no, revise or regenerate the figure.
