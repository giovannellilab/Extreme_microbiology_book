# Extreme environment chapter planning framework

## Purpose

Use this framework for chapters centred on a specific environment or environmental system.

Follow:

- `STYLE_GUIDE.md`
- `BOOK_CONVENTIONS.md`
- `FIGURE_STYLE.md`
- `CHAPTER_PRODUCTION_PROMPT.md`

This file is a planning framework, not a default table of contents.

Do not convert its questions into a sequence of generic chapter sections.

An environment chapter must develop an argument specific to its environment. Its architecture should emerge from the system’s defining processes, evidence and microbial ecology.

# 1. Central scientific logic

Environment chapters commonly connect:

geological, physical or hydrological process  
→ environmental structure and chemistry  
→ biological opportunities and constraints  
→ microbial activity and community organisation  
→ consequences within and beyond the habitat

This causal chain is a reasoning model.

It does not require one section for every step.

Several steps may be combined when the biology is best understood through a gradient, interface, disturbance event, representative site or ecological process.

The chapter should identify one memorable organising principle.

Examples include:

- mixing creates the habitable interface;
- freezing concentrates solutes while excluding cells;
- evaporation simultaneously reduces water availability and increases ionic stress;
- sediment burial changes transport faster than it removes chemical energy;
- mineral weathering continually recreates redox disequilibria;
- seasonal light and ice reorganise the same habitat through time.

The organising principle should be scientifically specific enough to guide selection and deletion.

It must not be a generic statement that environmental conditions affect microbial life.

# 2. Define the chapter’s unique contribution

Before proposing sections, state:

- the central scientific question;
- what the reader should understand after this chapter that is not taught elsewhere;
- which earlier chapters provide prerequisite knowledge;
- which concepts will be used without being re-taught;
- which neighbouring chapters own overlapping environments or mechanisms;
- which material is explicitly excluded.

An environment chapter should normally assume that readers already understand foundational concepts such as:

- redox reactions;
- electron donors and acceptors;
- chemolithotrophy;
- autotrophy and heterotrophy;
- carbon fixation;
- respiration and fermentation;
- basic thermodynamics;
- basic cellular structures.

Use these concepts to explain the environment.

Do not interrupt the environmental narrative to reconstruct their definitions or classifications.

If a brief reminder is essential, limit it to the minimum needed for the local argument and cross-reference the primary chapter.

# 3. Select the architecture from the science

Do not begin with a standard sequence of headings.

Choose an architecture that reflects how the environment is best understood.

Possible organising structures include the following.

## Gradient-centred

Useful when microbial life is organised primarily by a spatial or chemical gradient.

The chapter may move:

source or boundary condition  
→ interacting gradients  
→ microbial niches  
→ community interactions  
→ export or system-scale effects

## Interface-centred

Useful when biological activity depends on reactants, fluids or phases meeting.

The chapter may move among:

the separated endmembers  
→ the physical interface  
→ substrate transport  
→ microbial production  
→ community construction  
→ movement or transformation beyond the interface

## Disturbance-centred

Useful when environmental change, succession or episodic forcing defines the system.

The chapter may follow:

initial condition  
→ disturbance  
→ altered chemistry or transport  
→ microbial response  
→ succession or recovery  
→ longer-term consequences

## Habitat-mosaic

Useful when several connected microhabitats perform different ecological functions.

The chapter may compare:

physical connection  
→ local conditions  
→ characteristic processes  
→ exchange of cells and material  
→ consequences of connectivity

## Case-led

Useful when one exceptionally well-studied site, experiment or observation reveals the general mechanism.

The chapter may begin with the case, develop the mechanism from it, compare one contrasting system and then establish what can and cannot be generalised.

## Process-led

Useful when one microbial process gives the environment its scientific importance.

The chapter may follow that process from substrate production and transport through organisms, communities and larger-scale consequences.

These are possible architectures, not templates.

Do not combine all of them.

Choose the smallest structure that explains the environment coherently.

# 4. Requirements for every proposed section

For every major section in the Gate 1 plan, provide:

- a concise, subject-specific working title;
- the scientific question answered;
- the environment-specific mechanism or observation developed;
- the evidence anchor;
- the representative example, organism, site or dataset, if any;
- the concepts assumed from other chapters;
- the material explicitly excluded.

An evidence anchor may be:

- a landmark observation;
- a field measurement;
- a controlled experiment;
- a time series;
- a spatial transect;
- a representative site;
- a comparative study;
- a quantitative synthesis;
- a well-supported mechanistic model.

A broad review may establish the framework, but it is not by itself an evidence anchor for every section.

No section should exist solely to provide “background”.

Place essential background immediately before the scientific claim that needs it.

Delete background that does not change the reader’s interpretation of the biology.

# 5. Geological, physical and chemical context

Explain how the environment forms only to the depth needed to understand:

- habitat structure;
- resource supply;
- transport;
- gradients;
- disturbance;
- biological accessibility;
- microbial activity;
- community organisation;
- material export.

Geology, physics and chemistry are causal components, not scenery.

They must not become independent surveys inside a microbiology chapter.

For every substantial geological or geochemical passage, ask:

> Which later biological explanation depends on this information?

If no later explanation depends on it, remove or shorten the passage.

Do not catalogue:

- tectonic settings;
- mineral assemblages;
- fluid types;
- chemical species;
- physical extremes;
- site classifications.

Select only the contrasts needed for the chapter’s argument.

A comparison between environments or sites should normally vary a small number of dimensions with clear biological consequences.

# 6. Energetics and metabolism

Describe environmental energy supply through the actual substrates, transport processes and metabolisms relevant to the system.

Do not add a general metabolism tutorial.

Do not define redox reactions, chemolithotrophy, autotrophy, heterotrophy or carbon fixation unless the editor explicitly identifies the definition as necessary.

Do not list all theoretically possible donor–acceptor combinations.

Focus on:

- which substrates are supplied;
- where they meet;
- whether they remain biologically accessible;
- which organisms or communities use them;
- what evidence demonstrates the process;
- what limits the rate or distribution of activity.

Thermodynamic potential should be connected to the physical delivery of substrates and to evidence of realised activity.

Do not repeat generic distinctions between thermodynamic possibility, genomic potential, expression and measured rates unless a specific claim depends on that distinction.

Carbon-fixation pathways, respiratory pathways and named enzymes should appear only when they explain an environment-specific pattern.

# 7. Microbial ecology as the centre of gravity

Unless the editor explicitly defines another purpose, microbial ecology should remain the centre of gravity of an environment chapter.

Organise microbial material around questions such as:

- Where does biomass production occur?
- Which organisms or functional groups occupy the principal niches?
- How do cells gain and retain access to substrates?
- How do attachment, motility, symbiosis or syntrophy change that access?
- How do organisms modify their own environment?
- How do communities assemble, persist or recover?
- How do cells, substrates and products move among connected habitats?
- Which processes have been demonstrated in situ?
- What varies among sites, seasons or disturbance states?

Representative organisms must have a defined teaching role.

Possible roles include:

- revealing a mechanism;
- demonstrating a limit;
- connecting microbial metabolism to a host;
- showing ecological succession;
- illustrating convergent solutions;
- providing a historically important discovery.

Do not include organisms merely to demonstrate diversity.

Do not construct a taxonomic tour of the environment.

# 8. Spatial and temporal organisation

Gradients, interfaces, mixing, transport, disturbance and succession should be integrated into the main argument when they organise the ecology.

Do not automatically isolate them in a separate section.

Use a separate spatial or temporal section only when it provides a new level of understanding that is not already developed elsewhere.

Where possible, distinguish:

- bulk measurements from microscale conditions;
- endmember conditions from inhabited microenvironments;
- transient states from persistent habitats;
- transported cells from resident or growing populations;
- local patterns from general mechanisms.

State these distinctions at the point where they affect interpretation.

Do not turn them into repeated methodological disclaimers.

# 9. Biogeochemical and broader significance

Include larger-scale consequences only when the chapter can identify a concrete process.

Suitable material may include:

- measured production;
- transformation of a defined chemical species;
- retention or export of an element;
- contribution to a local or regional flux;
- modification of mineral or sediment properties;
- formation or consumption of a recognised reservoir;
- a demonstrated ecosystem effect.

Use direct, descriptive section titles.

For example:

- “Global biogeochemical impact of deep-sea hydrothermal vents”;
- “Methane consumption in anoxic sediments”;
- “Carbon export from polar sea ice”;
- “Sulphur transformations in acidic springs”.

Do not use titles that advertise caution or the author’s intended stance.

Avoid:

- “Significance without overstatement”;
- “Why this matters”;
- “What we can and cannot conclude”;
- “Beyond the local environment”.

Express uncertainty through the precision of the claims.

Do not create a biogeochemical section merely because the template mentions biogeochemistry.

If the larger-scale evidence is weak, a short bounded paragraph may be sufficient.

# 10. Research frontiers

A research-frontiers section is optional.

Include it only when the unresolved questions emerge directly from the chapter’s evidence and mechanisms.

Select a small number of specific questions.

A strong frontier identifies:

- what is unknown;
- why current evidence cannot resolve it;
- what scientific distinction matters;
- what observation or experiment could resolve it.

Avoid generic lists of methods, including routine catalogues of:

- metagenomics;
- metatranscriptomics;
- proteomics;
- stable isotopes;
- cultivation;
- imaging;
- sensors;
- modelling.

Mention a method only when it is necessary to explain why a particular question remains unresolved or how it could be tested.

Do not end the chapter with generic statements that more interdisciplinary research is needed.

# 11. Optional material

Include optional material only when it strengthens the central scientific argument.

## Discovery and exploration

Use historical material when it explains a change in scientific understanding.

Avoid discovery chronologies that do not affect interpretation.

## Representative site

Use a site when it makes a general mechanism concrete.

State which conclusions are site-specific.

Do not let a famous site become a substitute for the environmental framework.

## Methods and sampling

Discuss methods when access, sampling disturbance, spatial resolution or measurement design materially changes scientific interpretation.

Integrate methodological limitations with the relevant evidence.

Do not add a general methods section by default.

## Origin of life and astrobiology

Include only when there is a direct mechanistic connection.

Distinguish:

- observations from the modern environment;
- experimental evidence;
- reconstruction of past conditions;
- analogue reasoning;
- hypothesis;
- speculation.

Do not add these topics merely because the environment is extreme.

## Human relevance and applications

Include only when the relationship follows directly from the environmental microbiology.

Avoid application catalogues.

# 12. Figures

Plan figures around the scientific object or relationship that must be seen.

Suitable subjects include:

- a physical environment;
- a spatial gradient;
- a habitat interface;
- a representative organism–environment interaction;
- a measured comparison;
- a time series;
- a process that is difficult to understand from prose alone.

For every proposed major figure, specify:

- the scientific question;
- the main visual object;
- the evidence or source concepts;
- which features must be scientifically controlled;
- the intended production mode;
- successful book figures that provide a quality reference;
- visual approaches that are explicitly rejected.

Production mode must be one of:

- scientific illustration;
- photograph with restrained annotation;
- image-generated scientific illustration with vector/text overlay;
- quantitative plot;
- simple vector schematic;
- hybrid composition.

Environmental scenes should normally use scientific illustration, photography or a hybrid approach.

A pure vector schematic should be selected only when abstraction is scientifically preferable.

Do not use flat vector graphics merely because they are easier to generate.

Do not propose several figures that repeat the same gradient or causal chain.

# 13. Tables

Use a table only when exact comparison across repeated dimensions is easier to understand in rows and columns than in prose.

A table should not:

- repeat a figure;
- reproduce surrounding prose;
- catalogue organisms;
- catalogue metabolisms;
- store background information that did not fit elsewhere.

If the comparison is conceptual and visually spatial, prefer a figure.

If the table does not materially improve comparison, omit it.

# 14. Chapter opening and ending

Open with a chapter-specific scientific problem, observation, contradiction, experiment, field site or historical change.

The opening should establish the question that organises the chapter.

Avoid generic openings about:

- the environment being extreme;
- microorganisms being remarkably adaptable;
- the environment’s broad importance;
- the increasing availability of molecular methods.

The chapter does not require a conventional conclusion.

End where the scientific argument naturally ends.

Possible endings include:

- a synthesis of the central mechanism;
- the consequences of the environment beyond the habitat;
- a sharply defined unresolved question;
- Key concepts.

Do not add a generic summary merely to provide closure.

# 15. Key concepts

Key concepts should state what is distinctive about the environment.

They should not repeat foundational definitions.

Each item should help the reader reconstruct a causal relationship or interpret evidence.

Avoid generic statements such as:

- microorganisms exploit available energy;
- environmental conditions shape communities;
- molecular methods reveal microbial diversity;
- multiple factors interact;
- more research is needed.

If a key concept could be transferred unchanged to several chapters, rewrite or remove it.

# 16. Further reading

Provide a short curated set of useful entry points.

Select sources for distinct purposes, such as:

- environmental framework;
- defining mechanism;
- landmark observation;
- representative case;
- recent synthesis;
- unresolved frontier.

Do not reproduce the chapter bibliography.

Do not annotate sources with generic descriptions that merely restate their titles.

# 17. Gate 1 readiness test

An environment chapter plan is not ready for approval until the editor can answer yes to all of the following:

- Does the chapter have one recognisable organising principle?
- Is its unique contribution distinct from neighbouring chapters?
- Are prerequisite concepts identified?
- Are concepts that must not be re-taught explicitly listed?
- Does every major section answer an environment-specific question?
- Does every major section have an evidence anchor?
- Is geological and chemical context limited to what the biology uses?
- Is microbial ecology the centre of gravity?
- Are representative organisms and sites assigned clear teaching roles?
- Are headings descriptive rather than rhetorical or defensive?
- Are optional sections included only because the evidence requires them?
- Is every major figure tied to an approved production mode and quality reference?
- Could any proposed section be deleted without weakening the chapter’s unique argument?

If the answer to the final question is yes, delete or redesign that section before Gate 1 approval.
