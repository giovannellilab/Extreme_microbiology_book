# STYLE_GUIDE.md — Microbiology of Extreme Environments

## Purpose

This guide defines the scientific, pedagogical and writing style of *Microbiology of Extreme Environments*.

The book is an introductory but scientifically rigorous textbook for advanced undergraduate, MSc and early PhD students from microbiology, biology, chemistry, geology, Earth sciences, environmental sciences, oceanography, astrobiology and related disciplines.

Assume scientific literacy, but do not assume extensive training across all of these fields.

The book is not encyclopaedic.

Its purpose is to help readers understand mechanisms, connect disciplines, reason from evidence and continue learning.

“Introductory” applies to the book as a whole. It does not mean that every chapter should restart from basic definitions. Knowledge must accumulate across the book.

# 1. Book-level progression

Each important foundational concept should have one primary home.

Later chapters should use that concept rather than teach it again.

Before planning or drafting a chapter, identify:

- knowledge established by earlier chapters;
- concepts that require only a brief reminder;
- concepts that genuinely need new explanation;
- the scientific contribution unique to the current chapter.

Later chapters must not routinely redefine concepts such as:

- redox reactions;
- electron donors and acceptors;
- chemolithotrophy;
- autotrophy and heterotrophy;
- carbon fixation;
- chemiosmosis;
- Gibbs free energy;
- basic cellular structures;
- basic ecological terminology.

If an established concept is essential to the local argument, provide only the minimum orientation required for the paragraph to make sense, then cross-reference its primary chapter.

A brief reminder is normally one sentence, not a tutorial.

A chapter should be independently readable without being independently comprehensive.

# 2. Scientific and pedagogical approach

Prefer mechanisms, causal reasoning and mental models over catalogues of facts.

For extremophiles, explanations should normally follow:

environmental condition  
→ physicochemical consequence  
→ biological problem  
→ adaptation  
→ ecological or evolutionary consequence

For environments:

geological or physical process  
→ environmental chemistry  
→ energetic opportunities and constraints  
→ microbial processes  
→ community organisation  
→ biogeochemical consequences

For metabolism:

substrates and conditions  
→ chemical reaction  
→ thermodynamic and kinetic constraints  
→ molecular machinery  
→ biological function  
→ environmental significance

These sequences are reasoning tools, not mandatory section structures.

A reader should be able to reason beyond the examples explicitly presented.

For genuinely unfamiliar or difficult mechanisms:

1. establish the simplest causal model;
2. explain the mechanism;
3. connect it to evidence or a representative example;
4. add qualifications only where they materially affect interpretation.

Do not explain a familiar foundational mechanism merely because it appears in the causal chain.

Avoid fact stacking.

A strong section normally develops:

question or observation  
→ explanation  
→ evidence or case  
→ implication

The implication should advance the chapter. It should not merely repeat the preceding paragraph.

# 3. Scientific voice

The book should sound like an expert scientist explaining a difficult subject clearly to an intelligent interdisciplinary reader.

The tone should be:

- rigorous;
- direct;
- accessible;
- intellectually curious;
- confident where evidence is strong;
- appropriately qualified where evidence is limited;
- occasionally informal when this improves understanding.

It should not sound like:

- an encyclopaedia;
- a review article;
- a lecture transcript;
- a popular-science book;
- a study guide assembled from definitions;
- a risk-management document;
- generic AI-generated educational prose.

Use strong analogies when they genuinely improve understanding, but connect them to the precise scientific mechanism.

Do not use an analogy in place of an explanation.

Do not make the prose sound defensive merely to demonstrate caution.

Scientific restraint should be expressed through precise claims, not repeated warnings.

# 4. Source material and authorial voice

Lecture recordings, slides, notes, transcripts, existing drafts and curated literature are intellectual source material.

Do not mechanically polish or summarise them.

Preserve:

- reasoning;
- examples;
- conceptual sequence;
- teaching logic;
- memorable explanations;
- scientifically defensible analogies;
- distinctive intellectual perspective;
- the questions that motivated the original material.

Rewrite weak wording when necessary.

Do not replace distinctive teaching logic with generic textbook prose.

The author-curated source list is part of the intellectual identity of the chapter. It should shape the chapter’s architecture and choice of examples.

Inclusion of a source does not require a dedicated paragraph.

A source should appear because it supports a necessary claim, case study, historical shift or conceptual framework—not because it was present in the source pack.

# 5. Scope, specificity and depth

Include what the reader needs to understand the chapter’s subject, not everything known about it.

Prefer:

- principles over lists;
- mechanisms over catalogues;
- representative organisms over exhaustive taxonomy;
- representative pathways over every known variant;
- measured examples over generic claims;
- a few strong cases over collections of anecdotes.

Every major section must answer a question specific to the chapter.

Every substantive paragraph should contain at least one chapter-specific element, such as:

- a particular environment;
- a defined process;
- an organism with a clear teaching role;
- an observation;
- an experiment;
- a measured pattern;
- a quantitative value;
- a named case study;
- a mechanistic connection unique to the subject.

Apply the transfer test:

> Could this paragraph appear substantially unchanged in another chapter?

If yes:

- delete it;
- replace it with a cross-reference;
- move it to the chapter that owns the concept;
- or rewrite it around evidence and mechanisms specific to the current chapter.

Apply the deletion test:

> Would removing this paragraph reduce the reader’s ability to understand the chapter’s unique scientific argument?

If no, remove it.

Parallel mechanisms should be explained at comparable depth only when comparison serves the chapter.

Do not create symmetrical subsections merely because one topic received more space than another.

Chapter length must follow conceptual need.

There is no minimum word count.

Never add background, definitions, caveats, summaries or examples merely to reach an expected length.

A concise chapter with a strong scientific identity is preferable to a longer chapter diluted by generic explanation.

# 6. Language and prose

Use British English throughout.

Examples:

- behaviour;
- organisation;
- characterised;
- analyse;
- metabolise;
- optimise;
- metre;
- litre;
- centre;
- sulphur.

Do not alter spelling inside publication titles, gene names, database terms or quotations.

Use clear international English.

Prefer active constructions where natural.

Paragraphs should normally develop one coherent idea.

Use transitions to show scientific relationships, not simply to connect blocks of text.

Avoid:

- unnecessary passive constructions;
- very long paragraphs;
- excessive bullet lists;
- repetitive mini-conclusions;
- rhetorical filler;
- unnecessary adjectives;
- unsupported superlatives;
- restating the same distinction in several sections;
- generic bridge paragraphs;
- generic summaries of what “microorganisms” do;
- abstract prose unsupported by a concrete mechanism or example.

Avoid formulaic phrases such as:

- “It is important to note that…”;
- “Interestingly…”;
- “It is worth noting that…”;
- “In today’s rapidly evolving field…”;
- “In conclusion…”;
- “This highlights the importance of…”;
- “This underscores the complex interplay between…”;
- “A key point is that…”;
- “It is not merely X, but Y…”;
- “X should not be understood as…”;
- “Taken together, these findings demonstrate…” when no genuine synthesis follows.

Do not repeatedly use corrective constructions such as:

- “not X but Y”;
- “does not automatically mean”;
- “must not be confused with”;
- “these are related but not equivalent”.

Use such constructions only where a real and likely misconception would otherwise alter scientific interpretation.

# 7. Definitions and technical terminology

Introduce necessary technical vocabulary rather than avoiding it.

Define a term fully in the chapter that owns the concept.

In later chapters:

- use established terminology directly;
- add a short reminder only when needed;
- cross-reference the primary treatment;
- do not rebuild the conceptual taxonomy.

Do not interrupt a chapter-specific argument to explain general categories already established elsewhere.

For example, an environment chapter may identify the metabolisms occurring in that environment without re-explaining the difference among chemolithotrophy, autotrophy and heterotrophy.

Define abbreviations at first use unless universally familiar to the intended audience.

Do not introduce abbreviations that appear only once or twice.

Use italics for:

- genus and species;
- bacterial and archaeal genes;
- newly introduced technical terms where useful.

Use bold sparingly for memorable high-value conceptual anchors.

Do not use underlining for emphasis.

# 8. Evidence and uncertainty

Always distinguish among:

- established knowledge;
- current interpretation;
- hypothesis or proposed mechanism;
- speculation.

Do not turn tentative evidence into certainty.

Be especially careful with:

- limits of life;
- uncultivated organisms;
- deep biosphere activity;
- origin-of-life scenarios;
- astrobiology;
- environmental processes inferred only from genomic data.

The following are not equivalent:

- detection of biological material;
- survival;
- viability;
- metabolic activity;
- maintenance;
- growth;
- reproduction.

Claims must describe the endpoint actually demonstrated.

A detected gene does not by itself establish activity.

A measured reaction does not necessarily establish growth.

Recovery after exposure does not necessarily establish activity during exposure.

Apply these distinctions where they affect a specific scientific claim.

Do not insert a general evidence hierarchy into every chapter or section.

Do not repeatedly explain that genes, transcripts, proteins, rates and growth experiments provide different forms of evidence. State the relevant limitation locally and concisely.

A qualification belongs next to the claim it qualifies.

Do not accumulate caveats in standalone paragraphs merely to show methodological caution.

# 9. Taxonomy and biological nomenclature

Genus and species names are italicised:

*Escherichia coli*

*Methanopyrus kandleri*

*Sulfolobus acidocaldarius*

After first full use within a section, abbreviate the genus where unambiguous:

*Escherichia coli* → *E. coli*

Strain designations are not italicised:

*Escherichia coli* K-12

*Planococcus halocryophilus* Or1

Higher taxonomic ranks are normally not italicised:

Archaea  
Bacteria  
Eukarya  
Proteobacteria

Use current taxonomy where scientifically relevant, but do not disrupt readability merely to follow every nomenclatural revision.

When an important taxonomic change affects interpretation, mention the alternative or former name where useful.

Use *Candidatus* according to accepted convention.

# 10. Genes and proteins

For bacterial and archaeal genes, use lowercase italics:

*recA*  
*dsrA*  
*amoA*

Proteins are roman type:

RecA  
DsrA  
AmoA

Gene clusters or operons follow established nomenclature:

*amoABC*  
*dsrAB*  
*nifHDK*

Full protein names are roman:

DNA polymerase  
hydrogenase  
ATP synthase

Do not invent gene symbols.

For viruses, eukaryotes or other systems with established discipline-specific conventions, follow the accepted convention for that field.

# 11. Chemical notation, units and numbers

Use chemical formulae when they improve clarity:

CO₂  
H₂  
O₂  
CH₄  
H₂S  
NH₄⁺  
NO₃⁻  
SO₄²⁻  
Fe²⁺  
Fe³⁺

Use SI or SI-compatible units unless another disciplinary convention is more informative.

Use a space between value and unit:

10 °C  
5 mL  
100 MPa  
25 µm  
20 kJ mol⁻¹

Use:

37 °C

but:

310 K

Use an en dash for ranges:

20–30 °C  
5–10 µm  
pH 4–6

Use decimal points:

3.5  
0.25

Use a leading zero:

0.5

Do not report more precision than the evidence supports.

Use scientific notation where useful:

1.5 × 10⁶ cells mL⁻¹

Use:

5%

unless the publishing system later requires another convention.

For percentages describing concentration, state the basis when ambiguity matters.

# 12. Environmental and metabolic terminology

## Salinity

Do not treat salinity, NaCl concentration and ionic strength as equivalent.

Use the convention appropriate to the measurement and explain it when necessary.

## pH

Write:

pH 7  
pH 2.5  
pH 8–10

Remember that pH is logarithmic.

## Pressure

Use Pa and its multiples where practical:

100 kPa  
10 MPa  
100 MPa

Do not use depth as a synonym for hydrostatic pressure.

## Water activity

Use:

a_w

Define water activity in its primary conceptual treatment and briefly remind the reader elsewhere only when needed.

Do not treat water activity and water content as equivalent.

## Thermodynamics

Keep thermodynamics and kinetics conceptually separate.

Use ΔG for Gibbs free-energy change under the conditions being discussed.

Distinguish standard-state values from environmentally calculated values when this affects interpretation.

State the basis of energy quantities when necessary.

Do not describe a thermodynamically favourable reaction as necessarily fast.

Do not re-teach thermodynamic principles in every environment chapter.

## Redox

Use electron donor and electron acceptor where this improves clarity.

Remember:

- donor is oxidised;
- acceptor is reduced;
- oxidant accepts electrons;
- reductant donates electrons.

Use redox potential carefully and state reference conditions when quantitative interpretation depends on them.

Do not define a redox reaction in later chapters unless the editor has explicitly identified that definition as necessary.

## Metabolic terminology

Keep these dimensions separate:

Energy source:

- light;
- chemical reactions.

Electron donor:

- inorganic;
- organic.

Carbon source:

- inorganic carbon;
- organic carbon.

Phototroph, chemotroph, lithotroph, organotroph, autotroph and heterotroph describe different properties.

Do not equate chemolithotrophy with autotrophy.

This distinction should be taught fully in its primary metabolism chapter. Elsewhere, use the terms precisely without repeating the classification lesson.

## Extremophile terminology

Distinguish -phile from -tolerant.

A -phile has an optimum under the relevant condition.

A tolerant organism can withstand the condition while having its optimum elsewhere.

Operational category boundaries should not be presented as immutable biological divisions.

# 13. Representative organisms, history and applications

Representative organisms should illustrate mechanisms, discoveries, ecological roles or empirical limits.

They should not become taxonomic catalogues.

A representative organism should normally perform its main pedagogical role once. Later mentions should build on that role rather than repeat it.

There is no target number of representative organisms.

Historical material should be included when it explains how a concept, assumption, method or empirical limit changed.

Avoid chronologies that do not advance understanding.

Applications should grow naturally from the biology already explained.

Do not append catalogues of industrial or commercial uses.

Detailed biotechnology belongs primarily in the dedicated applications chapter.

# 14. Ecology, evolution and astrobiology

Ecological significance should be concrete.

Prefer:

- a process;
- a flux;
- a reservoir;
- a measured rate;
- a defined ecosystem effect;
- a robust quantitative estimate;
- a clearly bounded case study.

Avoid generic statements that microorganisms are “important”, “play a crucial role” or “contribute significantly”.

Use quantitative values only when supported by appropriate evidence.

Evolutionary arguments must distinguish:

- observed traits;
- comparative associations;
- inferred ancestral states;
- hypotheses about adaptation.

Do not treat a modern deep-branching organism as a preserved ancestor.

Astrobiological discussion must distinguish:

- terrestrial observations;
- experimentally demonstrated limits;
- plausible extrapolation;
- planetary constraints;
- speculation about extraterrestrial life.

An Earth analogue does not demonstrate extraterrestrial habitability or life.

Do not add astrobiology merely because the chapter concerns an extreme environment.

# 15. Chapter architecture, headings and cross-references

Each chapter should have a clear scientific identity.

Its sections should be organised around the subject rather than around a generic explanatory template.

Section titles should be:

- concise;
- descriptive;
- scientifically informative;
- understandable without reading the production plan;
- specific enough to distinguish the section from material elsewhere in the book.

Prefer headings such as:

- “Global biogeochemical impact of deep-sea hydrothermal vents”;
- “Microbial primary production in vent mixing zones”;
- “Subseafloor and plume connectivity”;
- “Membrane adaptation at high temperature”.

Avoid headings that describe:

- the writer’s intention;
- the desired tone;
- evidence-management strategy;
- caution against overstatement;
- the process of explaining;
- generic importance.

Avoid headings such as:

- “Why this matters”;
- “What we can and cannot say”;
- “Significance without inflated budgets”;
- “A note of caution”;
- “Putting it all together”;
- “Beyond the basics”;
- “A complex and dynamic picture”.

Rhetorical headings are acceptable only when the question itself is scientifically productive and the section directly answers it.

Chapter openings should establish an intellectual problem, observation, contradiction, experiment, case or historical change.

Avoid generic openings about the broad importance or ubiquity of the topic.

Cross-references should prevent unnecessary repetition.

Before referring elsewhere, provide only enough information for the local claim to make sense.

Prefer:

“As discussed in Chapter 10, reaction yield depends on environmental conditions; here the relevant variable is the mixing rate between vent fluid and seawater.”

Avoid:

“Redox reactions transfer electrons from donors to acceptors…” followed by a general tutorial already provided elsewhere.

Do not add a conclusion merely because chapters conventionally have conclusions.

Use one only when it produces a genuine synthesis.

# 16. Figures, tables and boxes

Figures are explanatory tools, not decoration.

Detailed visual rules are defined in `FIGURE_STYLE.md`.

Use figures when they communicate a mechanism, gradient, spatial relationship, comparison or dataset more effectively than prose.

Tables are useful for concise comparison or structured evidence.

Do not use large tables as substitutes for explanation.

Do not create a table that merely repeats a figure and the surrounding prose.

Boxes should be used sparingly for material that benefits from temporary depth without breaking the main narrative.

Useful box types include:

- concept;
- case study;
- methods;
- frontier;
- application.

Do not create boxes merely to store material that does not fit the chapter.

# 17. Citations and Further Reading

The book is a textbook, not a review article.

Cite:

- quantitative claims;
- landmark discoveries;
- named mechanisms where attribution matters;
- controversial claims;
- specific limits of life;
- rapidly developing areas;
- reproduced or adapted data.

Do not cite every sentence mechanically.

Prefer primary literature for specific experiments and authoritative reviews for broad synthesis.

Never invent a citation.

References are stored in the central BibTeX database.

Every substantial chapter should end with a curated Further Reading section.

Further Reading should contain a small number of useful entry points into the subject, not a duplicate of the bibliography.

Do not create generic annotations that merely restate article titles.

# 18. Markdown

Use hierarchical Markdown headings:

# NN. Chapter title

## Major section

### Subsection

#### Minor subsection

Do not skip levels unnecessarily.

The visible H1 must contain the chapter number and title and agree with the canonical chapter identity.

Use relative internal links and figure paths.

Use LaTeX-compatible notation for equations where required.

Do not use Markdown structure to compensate for weak prose.

Excessive headings, bullet lists or callout boxes fragment the chapter rather than clarify it.

# 19. AI-assisted work

AI may help with:

- organising source material;
- drafting and restructuring;
- identifying duplication;
- checking terminology;
- reference auditing;
- figure planning;
- internal consistency;
- editorial revision.

AI is not a scientific source.

AI-generated references must be verified.

AI-generated scientific claims extending beyond supplied material must be checked against reliable literature.

AI must not silently expand a chapter to make it appear comprehensive or complete.

AI must not add definitions, transitions, caveats, examples, conclusions or summaries merely to increase length or smooth the outline.

AI-generated prose must be checked for:

- generic explanation;
- textbook-reset behaviour;
- repeated definitions;
- repeated evidence disclaimers;
- predictable paragraph templates;
- unnecessary conceptual taxonomies;
- abstract claims without concrete evidence;
- headings that expose production instructions;
- loss of the author’s distinctive teaching logic.

Warning signs of generic AI prose include:

- paragraphs that can be moved unchanged to another chapter;
- several consecutive paragraphs with the same explanatory structure;
- repeated “not X but Y” formulations;
- repeated distinctions among categories already taught elsewhere;
- generic claims followed by generic caveats;
- broad statements about complexity, importance or future research;
- conclusion sentences that merely restate the paragraph;
- prose that explains how cautiously the subject will be discussed rather than discussing the subject.

When these signs appear, deletion is preferred to cosmetic rewriting.

Preserve the intellectual character of the source material rather than replacing it with generic textbook prose.

# 20. Editorial priority

When rules conflict, use this order:

1. scientific accuracy;
2. correct representation of evidence and uncertainty;
3. chapter-specific intellectual value;
4. conceptual clarity;
5. pedagogical usefulness;
6. consistency with the book’s cumulative structure;
7. preservation of authorial voice;
8. conciseness;
9. stylistic consistency.

No style rule should force scientifically misleading language.

A chapter is not successful merely because it is accurate, complete, well formatted and free of mechanical errors.

It must also teach something specific, retain an identifiable intellectual voice and justify every section it contains.
