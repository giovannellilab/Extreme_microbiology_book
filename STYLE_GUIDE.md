# STYLE_GUIDE.md — Microbiology of Extreme Environments

## Purpose

This guide defines the scientific, pedagogical and writing style of *Microbiology of Extreme Environments*.

The book is an introductory but scientifically rigorous textbook for advanced undergraduate, MSc and early PhD students from microbiology, biology, chemistry, geology, Earth sciences, environmental sciences, oceanography, astrobiology and related disciplines.

Assume scientific literacy, but do not assume extensive microbiology training.

The book is not encyclopaedic.

Its purpose is to explain enough for the reader to understand, reason and continue learning.

# 1. Scientific and pedagogical approach

Prefer mechanisms, causal reasoning and mental models over catalogues of facts.

For extremophiles, explanations should normally follow:

environmental condition
→ physicochemical consequence
→ biological problem
→ adaptation
→ ecological or evolutionary consequence

For environments:

geological or physical process
→ environmental chemistry
→ energetic opportunities and constraints
→ microbial metabolisms
→ communities
→ biogeochemical consequences

For metabolism:

substrates and conditions
→ chemical reaction
→ thermodynamic and kinetic constraints
→ molecular machinery
→ biological function
→ environmental significance

A reader should be able to reason beyond the examples explicitly presented.

Explain the intuitive problem first, then introduce the technical terminology.

For difficult mechanisms:

1. establish the simplest causal model;
2. explain the mechanism;
3. add qualifications and exceptions only where they matter.

Avoid fact stacking.

A strong section normally follows:

question or problem
→ explanation
→ evidence or examples
→ synthesis

# 2. Scientific voice

The book should sound like an expert scientist explaining a difficult subject clearly to an intelligent interdisciplinary reader.

The tone should be:

- rigorous
- direct
- accessible
- intellectually curious
- occasionally informal when this improves understanding

It should not sound like:

- an encyclopaedia
- a review article
- a lecture transcript
- a popular-science book
- generic AI-generated educational prose

Use strong analogies when they genuinely improve understanding, but always connect them to the precise scientific mechanism.

Do not use an analogy in place of an explanation.

# 3. Source material and authorial voice

Lecture recordings, slides, notes, transcripts, existing drafts and curated literature are source material.

Do not mechanically polish them.

Preserve:

- reasoning
- examples
- conceptual sequence
- teaching logic
- memorable explanations
- scientifically defensible analogies
- distinctive intellectual perspective

Rewrite weak wording when necessary.

Do not replace distinctive teaching logic with generic textbook prose.

The author-curated source list is part of the intellectual identity of the chapter. It should inform chapter construction, but inclusion of a source does not require dedicated space in the final text.

# 4. Scope and depth

Include what the reader needs to understand the subject, not everything known about it.

Prefer:

- principles over lists
- mechanisms over catalogues
- representative organisms over exhaustive taxonomy
- representative pathways over every known variant
- a few strong examples over collections of anecdotes

If a sentence could be moved unchanged into several other extremophile chapters, either:

- make it specific;
- move the underlying concept to its primary chapter;
- or remove it.

Parallel mechanisms should normally be explained at comparable depth unless the evidence justifies otherwise.

Chapter length should follow conceptual need rather than a fixed target.

Indicative ranges:

- foundational/conceptual: 4,000–7,000 words
- extremophile: 4,000–6,000 words
- environment: 3,000–7,000 words
- synthesis: 5,000–8,000 words

These are guidelines, not requirements.

# 5. Language and prose

Use British English throughout.

Examples:

- behaviour
- organisation
- characterised
- analyse
- metabolise
- optimise
- metre
- litre
- centre
- sulphur

Do not alter spelling inside publication titles, gene names, database terms or quotations.

Use clear international English.

Prefer active constructions where natural.

Paragraphs should normally develop one coherent idea.

Avoid:

- unnecessary passive constructions
- very long paragraphs
- excessive bullet lists
- repetitive mini-conclusions
- rhetorical filler
- unnecessary adjectives
- unsupported superlatives

Avoid formulaic phrases such as:

- “It is important to note that…”
- “Interestingly…”
- “It is worth noting that…”
- “In today's rapidly evolving field…”
- “In conclusion…”

Use them only when they genuinely add meaning.

# 6. Evidence and uncertainty

Always distinguish among:

- established knowledge
- current interpretation
- hypothesis or proposed mechanism
- speculation

Do not turn tentative evidence into certainty.

Be especially careful with:

- limits of life
- uncultivated organisms
- deep biosphere activity
- origin-of-life scenarios
- astrobiology
- environmental processes inferred only from genomic data

The following are not equivalent:

- detection of biological material
- survival
- viability
- metabolic activity
- maintenance
- growth
- reproduction

Claims must describe the endpoint actually demonstrated.

A detected gene does not by itself establish activity.

A measured reaction does not necessarily establish growth.

Recovery after exposure does not necessarily establish activity during exposure.

# 7. Taxonomy and biological nomenclature

Genus and species names are italicised:

*Escherichia coli*

*Methanopyrus kandleri*

*Sulfolobus acidocaldarius*

After first full use within a section, abbreviate the genus where unambiguous:

*Escherichia coli* → *E. coli*

Strain designations are not italicised:

*Escherichia coli* K-12

*Planococcus halocryophilus* Or1

Higher taxonomic ranks are normally not italicised:

Archaea
Bacteria
Eukarya
Proteobacteria

Use current taxonomy where scientifically relevant, but do not disrupt readability merely to follow every nomenclatural revision.

When an important taxonomic change affects interpretation, mention the alternative or former name where useful.

Use *Candidatus* according to accepted convention.

# 8. Genes and proteins

For bacterial and archaeal genes, use lowercase italics:

*recA*
*dsrA*
*amoA*

Proteins are roman type:

RecA
DsrA
AmoA

Gene clusters or operons follow established nomenclature:

*amoABC*
*dsrAB*
*nifHDK*

Full protein names are roman:

DNA polymerase
hydrogenase
ATP synthase

Do not invent gene symbols.

For viruses, eukaryotes or other systems with established discipline-specific conventions, follow the accepted convention for that field.

# 9. Chemical notation, units and numbers

Use chemical formulae when they improve clarity:

CO₂
H₂
O₂
CH₄
H₂S
NH₄⁺
NO₃⁻
SO₄²⁻
Fe²⁺
Fe³⁺

Use SI or SI-compatible units unless another disciplinary convention is more informative.

Use a space between value and unit:

10 °C
5 mL
100 MPa
25 µm
20 kJ mol⁻¹

Use:

37 °C

but:

310 K

Use an en dash for ranges:

20–30 °C
5–10 µm
pH 4–6

Use decimal points:

3.5
0.25

Use a leading zero:

0.5

Do not report more precision than the evidence supports.

Use scientific notation where useful:

1.5 × 10⁶ cells mL⁻¹

Use:

5%

unless the publishing system later requires another convention.

For percentages describing concentration, state the basis when ambiguity matters.

# 10. Environmental and metabolic terminology

## Salinity

Do not treat salinity, NaCl concentration and ionic strength as equivalent.

Use the convention appropriate to the measurement and explain it when necessary.

## pH

Write:

pH 7
pH 2.5
pH 8–10

Remember that pH is logarithmic.

## Pressure

Use Pa and its multiples where practical:

100 kPa
10 MPa
100 MPa

Do not use depth as a synonym for hydrostatic pressure.

## Water activity

Use:

a_w

Define water activity at first substantive use.

Do not treat water activity and water content as equivalent.

## Thermodynamics

Keep thermodynamics and kinetics conceptually separate.

Use ΔG for Gibbs free-energy change under the conditions being discussed.

Distinguish standard-state values from environmentally calculated values when this affects interpretation.

State the basis of energy quantities when necessary.

Do not describe a thermodynamically favourable reaction as necessarily fast.

## Redox

Use electron donor and electron acceptor where this improves clarity.

Remember:

- donor is oxidised
- acceptor is reduced
- oxidant accepts electrons
- reductant donates electrons

Use redox potential carefully and state reference conditions when quantitative interpretation depends on them.

## Metabolic terminology

Keep these dimensions separate:

Energy source:
- light
- chemical reactions

Electron donor:
- inorganic
- organic

Carbon source:
- inorganic carbon
- organic carbon

Phototroph, chemotroph, lithotroph, organotroph, autotroph and heterotroph describe different properties.

Do not equate chemolithotrophy with autotrophy.

## Extremophile terminology

Distinguish -phile from -tolerant.

A -phile has an optimum under the relevant condition.

A tolerant organism can withstand the condition while having its optimum elsewhere.

Operational category boundaries should not be presented as immutable biological divisions.

# 11. Terminology, acronyms and emphasis

Introduce necessary technical vocabulary rather than avoiding it.

Explain the concept first where necessary, then introduce the term and use it consistently.

Define abbreviations at first use unless universally familiar to the intended audience.

Do not introduce abbreviations that appear only once or twice.

Use italics for:

- genus and species
- bacterial and archaeal genes
- newly introduced technical terms where useful

Use bold sparingly for memorable high-value conceptual anchors.

Do not use underlining for emphasis.

# 12. Representative organisms, history and applications

Representative organisms should illustrate mechanisms, discoveries, ecological roles or empirical limits.

They should not become taxonomic catalogues.

A representative organism should normally perform its main pedagogical role once. Later mentions should build on that role rather than repeat it.

For extremophile chapters, approximately 3–6 memorable organisms is often appropriate, but this is not a quota.

Historical material should be included when it explains how a concept, assumption, method or limit changed.

Avoid chronologies that do not advance understanding.

Applications should grow naturally from the biology already explained.

Do not append catalogues of industrial or commercial uses.

Detailed biotechnology belongs primarily in the dedicated applications chapter.

# 13. Ecology, evolution and astrobiology

Ecological significance should be concrete.

Prefer:

- a process
- a flux
- a reservoir
- a measured rate
- a defined ecosystem effect
- a robust quantitative estimate

over generic statements that microorganisms are “important”.

Use quantitative values only when supported by appropriate evidence.

Evolutionary arguments must distinguish:

- observed traits
- comparative associations
- inferred ancestral states
- hypotheses about adaptation

Do not treat a modern deep-branching organism as a preserved ancestor.

Astrobiological discussion must distinguish:

- terrestrial observations
- experimentally demonstrated limits
- plausible extrapolation
- planetary constraints
- speculation about extraterrestrial life

An Earth analogue does not demonstrate extraterrestrial habitability or life.

# 14. Chapters and cross-references

Each chapter should be independently readable but does not need to be independently comprehensive.

Foundational concepts should have a primary home.

Later chapters may briefly explain the concept as needed for the local argument and then cross-reference the fuller treatment.

Before referring elsewhere, provide enough explanation for the current paragraph to make sense.

Prefer:

“As discussed in Chapter 10, the energetic yield of a reaction depends strongly on environmental conditions.”

over:

“See Chapter 10.”

Chapter openings should establish an intellectual problem, observation, contradiction, example or historical event.

Avoid generic openings.

Do not add a conclusion merely because chapters conventionally have conclusions.

Use one only when it helps integrate the chapter or identify what the reader should now understand.

# 15. Figures, tables and boxes

Figures are explanatory tools, not decoration.

Detailed visual rules are defined in `FIGURE_STYLE.md`.

Use figures when they communicate a mechanism, gradient, spatial relationship, comparison or dataset more effectively than prose.

Tables are useful for concise comparison or structured evidence.

Do not use large tables as substitutes for explanation.

Boxes should be used sparingly for material that benefits from temporary depth without breaking the main narrative.

Useful box types include:

- concept
- case study
- methods
- frontier
- application

Do not create boxes merely to store material that does not fit the chapter.

# 16. Citations and Further Reading

The book is a textbook, not a review article.

Cite:

- quantitative claims
- landmark discoveries
- named mechanisms where attribution matters
- controversial claims
- specific limits of life
- rapidly developing areas
- reproduced or adapted data

Do not cite every sentence mechanically.

Prefer primary literature for specific experiments and authoritative reviews for broad synthesis.

Never invent a citation.

References are stored in the central BibTeX database.

Every substantial chapter should end with a curated Further Reading section.

Further Reading should normally contain a small number of useful entry points into the subject, not a duplicate of the bibliography.

# 17. Markdown

Use hierarchical Markdown headings:

# NN. Chapter title

## Major section

### Subsection

#### Minor subsection

Do not skip levels unnecessarily.

The visible H1 must contain the chapter number and title and agree with the canonical chapter identity.

Use relative internal links and figure paths.

Use LaTeX-compatible notation for equations where required.

# 18. AI-assisted work

AI may help with:

- organising source material
- drafting and restructuring
- identifying duplication
- checking terminology
- reference auditing
- figure planning
- internal consistency
- editorial revision

AI is not a scientific source.

AI-generated references must be verified.

AI-generated scientific claims extending beyond supplied material must be checked against reliable literature.

AI must not silently expand a chapter merely to make it appear comprehensive.

Preserve the intellectual character of the source material rather than replacing it with generic textbook prose.

# 19. Editorial priority

When rules conflict, use this order:

1. scientific accuracy
2. correct representation of evidence and uncertainty
3. conceptual clarity
4. pedagogical usefulness
5. consistency with the book's intellectual framework
6. conciseness
7. stylistic consistency

No style rule should force scientifically misleading language.
