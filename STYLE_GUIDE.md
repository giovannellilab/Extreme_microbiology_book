# Style Guide — *Microbiology of Extreme Environments*

This document defines the editorial, scientific, linguistic and formatting conventions for *Microbiology of Extreme Environments*.

The book is a living, open textbook. These guidelines apply to all chapters, boxes, figures, tables and substantial contributions, whether written by the core editorial team, external contributors or with the assistance of AI tools.

The objective is consistency without forcing all contributors into an identical voice. Scientific accuracy, conceptual clarity and pedagogical usefulness take precedence over stylistic uniformity.

# 1. Purpose of the book

*Microbiology of Extreme Environments* is an introductory but scientifically rigorous textbook about microbial life under extreme conditions and in extreme environments.

The book is not intended to be encyclopaedic. Its purpose is to:

* explain the fundamental principles needed to understand microbial life in extreme environments;
* connect environmental physics and chemistry to microbial physiology, metabolism, ecology and evolution;
* provide enough microbiological background for readers entering from adjacent disciplines;
* introduce important organisms, environments and case studies without attempting exhaustive coverage;
* provide clear routes into the primary and review literature for readers who want to go deeper.

A chapter should contain what is necessary to understand its subject, not everything that is known about it.

The book should remain extensible. Particularly in the section on extreme environments, strong new chapters may be added in future versions when appropriate expertise and source material become available.

# 2. Intended audience

The primary audience consists of advanced undergraduate, MSc and early PhD students.

Readers may come from:

* microbiology and biology;
* biotechnology;
* chemistry and biochemistry;
* geology and Earth sciences;
* environmental sciences;
* oceanography;
* planetary science and astrobiology;
* engineering or related interdisciplinary fields.

Assume scientific literacy, but do not assume extensive previous training in microbiology.

Do not explain general science from first principles unless this is necessary to understand the biological concept being discussed.

Conversely, do not assume that a geologist or chemist already knows concepts such as chemiosmosis, horizontal gene transfer, ribosomal RNA phylogeny or the distinction between respiration and fermentation.

# 3. General pedagogical philosophy

## 3.1 Explain mechanisms rather than catalogue facts

Whenever possible, explanations should follow a causal structure.

For extremophiles:

**environmental condition → physicochemical consequence → biological problem → adaptation → ecological or evolutionary consequence**

For environments:

**geological/physical process → environmental chemistry → energetic opportunities and constraints → metabolisms → microbial communities → biogeochemical consequences**

For metabolism:

**substrates and conditions → chemical reaction → energetic constraints → molecular machinery → biological function → environmental significance**

Prefer:

> As temperature decreases, lipid bilayers become increasingly ordered and less fluid. This interferes with transport, membrane protein function and energy conservation. Psychrophiles counteract this effect by modifying membrane composition, commonly increasing the abundance of unsaturated or shorter-chain fatty acids.

over:

> Psychrophiles contain more unsaturated fatty acids.

## 3.2 Teach mental models

A chapter should enable the reader to reason beyond the examples explicitly presented.

After reading about psychrophiles, a reader should be able to predict some of the problems experienced by a cell at low temperature and infer plausible adaptations.

After reading about hydrothermal vents, a reader should be able to connect geological processes and fluid chemistry with the metabolisms expected at fluid–seawater interfaces.

This ability to reason is more important than memorising lists.

## 3.3 Introduce technical terminology rather than avoiding it

Do not remove necessary scientific vocabulary in the name of accessibility.

Instead:

1. explain the underlying idea in accessible language;
2. introduce the technical term;
3. use that term consistently thereafter.

Important terms should normally be defined once rather than repeatedly.

For interdisciplinary readers, explain the intuitive physical or chemical consequence before introducing specialist terminology. Do not assume microbiology, biochemistry or physical-chemistry knowledge beyond material already established in the book.

For a complex mechanism, establish the simplest causal model first. Add exceptions and qualifications only after the core mechanism is clear.

## 3.4 Use questions to introduce genuine conceptual problems

Questions are encouraged when they reflect the reasoning used to understand a subject.

Examples:

> What makes an environment extreme?

> Why does life need a membrane?

> How can microorganisms remain active below the freezing point of pure water?

> How much energy must a reaction provide before a microorganism can use it?

Avoid rhetorical questions that merely decorate the prose.

## 3.5 Distinguish levels of biological evidence

Particular care must be taken when discussing the limits of life.

The following are not interchangeable:

* detection of biological material;
* survival;
* viability;
* metabolic activity;
* maintenance;
* growth;
* reproduction.

For example, detection of DNA at −40 °C does not demonstrate microbial growth at −40 °C.

Claims must describe the level of evidence actually demonstrated.

# 4. Scientific voice

The book should sound like an expert scientist explaining a subject clearly to an intelligent interdisciplinary reader.

The tone should be:

* rigorous;
* direct;
* accessible;
* intellectually curious;
* occasionally informal when this improves understanding.

It should not sound like:

* a review article;
* an encyclopaedia;
* lecture transcription;
* a popular-science book;
* generic AI-generated educational prose.

Strong analogies used in teaching may be retained when they genuinely clarify a mechanism.

Examples include describing membranes as biological batteries or electron bifurcation using a pulley analogy. Such analogies must be followed by the scientifically precise explanation.

Analogy must never replace mechanism.

# 5. Treatment of source material

Lecture recordings, transcripts, slides, notes and existing drafts are **source material**, not text that must be preserved verbatim.

When converting teaching material into a chapter:

1. identify the scientific argument;
2. identify useful examples, analogies and conceptual sequences;
3. verify the scientific claims;
4. restructure the material according to the chapter's pedagogical purpose;
5. write new prose where necessary.

Do not mechanically polish transcripts.

Preserve the **reasoning and teaching logic**, not necessarily the original sentences.

Existing contributions from chapter authors should similarly be preserved at the level of scientific and intellectual contribution even when substantial rewriting is necessary.

# 6. Depth and scope

The book should provide enough depth for genuine understanding without attempting exhaustive coverage.

Prefer:

* principles over lists;
* mechanisms over catalogues;
* representative organisms over exhaustive taxonomy;
* representative pathways over every known variant;
* selected high-value examples over collections of anecdotes.

Before completing a chapter, identify sentences that could be copied unchanged into another extremophile chapter. Delete them, move the underlying concept to a foundational chapter, or make them specific to the environmental parameter being explained.

Do not remove every memorable lecture phenomenon in pursuit of a minimal mechanism-only account. Retain a small number of scientifically sound examples when they improve understanding, scientific relevance or the intellectual character of the course.

When several mechanisms are presented as parallel strategies, explain them at comparable conceptual resolution unless the evidence provides a clear reason for asymmetry.

If additional material is valuable but disrupts the central narrative, consider:

* an in-depth box;
* a table;
* a figure;
* a case study;
* the Further Reading section.

Chapter length should follow conceptual need rather than a fixed word count.

Indicative ranges are:

* foundational/conceptual chapter: 4,000–7,000 words;
* extremophile chapter: 4,000–6,000 words;
* environment chapter: 3,000–7,000 words;
* synthesis chapter: 5,000–8,000 words;
* in-depth box: 500–1,500 words.

These are guidelines, not requirements.

# 7. Language

Use **British English** throughout.

Examples:

* behaviour, not behavior;
* organisation, not organization;
* characterised, not characterized;
* sulphur, not sulfur, except where an official chemical name, database term, gene/protein name, quotation or title uses another spelling;
* metre, litre, centre;
* analyse, metabolise, optimise.

Do not alter spelling inside titles of cited publications.

Use clear international English and avoid unnecessary idioms.

# 8. Sentence and paragraph style

Prefer active constructions where natural.

Prefer:

> Cells maintain the proton gradient by…

over:

> The proton gradient is maintained by the cells through…

Paragraphs should normally develop one coherent idea.

Avoid very long paragraphs that combine unrelated concepts.

Do not fragment continuous explanations into excessive bullet lists.

Avoid generic filler such as:

* “It is important to note that…”
* “Interestingly…”
* “It should be mentioned that…”
* “It is worth noting that…”
* “In today's rapidly evolving scientific landscape…”
* “In conclusion, it can therefore be seen that…”

Use these constructions only when they genuinely add meaning.

Do not add repetitive mini-conclusions after every section.

# 9. Scientific uncertainty

Clearly distinguish:

**Established knowledge**
Supported by extensive evidence or broad scientific consensus.

**Current interpretation**
Well supported but potentially subject to revision.

**Hypothesis or proposed mechanism**
Plausible but incompletely established.

**Speculation**
Possible but currently poorly constrained.

Useful formulations include:

> Experimental evidence indicates that…

> These observations suggest that…

> One proposed mechanism is…

> Whether this represents active growth remains unresolved.

> The available evidence does not yet distinguish between…

Avoid presenting frontier research as established fact.

This is particularly important for:

* limits of life;
* deep biosphere estimates;
* origin-of-life scenarios;
* astrobiology;
* uncultivated microorganisms;
* environmental activity inferred only from genomic data.

# 10. Taxonomic nomenclature

## 10.1 Genus and species

Scientific genus and species names are italicised.

Examples:

*Escherichia coli*
*Methanopyrus kandleri*
*Sulfolobus acidocaldarius*

The genus name begins with a capital letter and the species epithet with a lowercase letter.

After the first full use within a section, the genus may normally be abbreviated where ambiguity is impossible:

*Escherichia coli* → *E. coli*

Do not begin a sentence with an abbreviated genus name if this can reasonably be avoided.

## 10.2 Higher taxonomic ranks

Names above the genus level are normally **not italicised** in running text.

Examples:

Archaea
Bacteria
Eukarya
Proteobacteria
Methanobacteriales

Use current accepted taxonomy where relevant, but do not repeatedly change familiar terminology merely to follow every recent nomenclatural revision. Where an important taxonomic change affects interpretation, mention both names if useful.

## 10.3 Common group names

Common biological group names are lower case unless they contain a proper noun.

Examples:

archaea
bacteria
methanogens
cyanobacteria
sulphate reducers
thermophiles
psychrophiles

Use **Archaea**, **Bacteria** and **Eukarya** when explicitly referring to the domains.

## 10.4 Strains

Strain designations are not italicised.

Examples:

*Escherichia coli* K-12
*Thermococcus kodakarensis* KOD1
*Planococcus halocryophilus* Or1

## 10.5 Candidatus

Use *Candidatus* according to accepted taxonomic convention.

Example:

*Candidatus Desulforudis audaxviator*

Do not abbreviate *Candidatus* on first use.

# 11. Gene and protein nomenclature

Consistency is essential.

For bacterial and archaeal genes, gene symbols are written in **lowercase italics**.

Example:

*prtC*

The corresponding protein is written in roman type with an initial capital letter:

PrtC

Thus:

> The *prtC* gene encodes the PrtC protein.

When gene names consist of several letters and a capital suffix:

*recA* → RecA
*dsrA* → DsrA
*coxL* → CoxL
*amoA* → AmoA

Do not italicise protein names.

Full descriptive protein names are written in roman type:

DNA polymerase
hydrogenase
dissimilatory sulphite reductase
ATP synthase

Gene clusters or operons should follow established nomenclature:

*amoABC*
*dsrAB*
*nifHDK*

Where locus tags rather than gene names are used, follow the original database/publication convention and do not italicise unless nomenclatural standards explicitly require it.

Mutant genotypes should use standard genetic notation where relevant.

Example:

Δ*prtC*

Do not invent gene symbols when no standard symbol exists.

For eukaryotic genes, viruses or organisms with established discipline-specific nomenclature, follow the accepted convention of that research community and define any departure from the bacterial/archaeal rule where necessary.

# 12. Viruses

Virus nomenclature should follow current ICTV conventions where possible.

Formal virus species names and common virus names should not be confused.

When taxonomic detail is not central to the discussion, prefer the terminology most useful to the reader rather than unnecessary formal classification.

Names of viral genes and proteins should follow the established nomenclature used for the particular system.

# 13. Chemical nomenclature

Use chemical formulae when they improve clarity:

CO₂
H₂
O₂
CH₄
H₂S
NH₄⁺
NO₃⁻
SO₄²⁻
Fe²⁺
Fe³⁺

Use appropriate subscripts and superscripts in final rendered text.

The first occurrence may include the full chemical name when useful:

> hydrogen sulphide (H₂S)

Thereafter use the formula if this improves readability.

Oxidation states may be written using either ionic notation or Roman numerals depending on context:

Fe²⁺
Fe(II)

Use one style consistently within the immediate discussion.

Reaction equations must be chemically balanced unless explicitly presented as simplified conceptual schemes.

# 14. Units and numerical conventions

Use SI units and SI-compatible units throughout unless a field-specific convention is more informative.

Examples:

m
km
µm
nm
kg
g
mg
µg
L
mL
µL
mol
mmol
µmol
Pa
kPa
MPa
J
kJ
W

Use a space between the numerical value and unit:

10 °C
5 mL
100 MPa
25 µm
20 kJ mol⁻¹

Do not write:

10°C
5ml
100MPa

The degree symbol is used for temperature:

37 °C

Kelvin is written without a degree symbol:

310 K

Use non-breaking spaces between values and units where supported by the publishing system.

## 14.1 Ranges

Use an en dash for numerical ranges:

20–30 °C
5–10 µm
pH 4–6

Avoid constructions such as “between 20–30 °C”. Use either:

> between 20 and 30 °C

or:

> 20–30 °C

## 14.2 Decimal notation

Use a decimal point:

3.5
0.25

Use a leading zero for values smaller than one:

0.5, not .5

Very large or small values may use scientific notation:

1.5 × 10⁶ cells mL⁻¹

## 14.3 Significant figures

Do not report more precision than the underlying measurement or source justifies.

## 14.4 Percentages

Use:

5%

with no space unless the final typesetting system or publisher requires another convention.

For concentrations, state clearly whether percentages are w/v, v/v or another basis when ambiguity matters.

# 15. Salinity

Avoid ambiguous use of percentages when describing environmental salinity.

Use the convention appropriate to the original measurement and explain it when necessary.

Where practical, prefer clearly defined quantities such as:

g kg⁻¹
mol L⁻¹
practical salinity values where historically appropriate

Do not silently treat salinity, NaCl concentration and ionic strength as equivalent.

# 16. pH

Write:

pH 7
pH 2.5
pH 8–10

Do not write “pH = 7” in ordinary prose unless presented as an equation or experimental condition where the equality notation is useful.

Remember that pH is logarithmic. Avoid language implying that pH 4 is simply “twice as acidic” as pH 8.

# 17. Pressure

Pressure should normally be given in Pa and its multiples:

100 kPa
10 MPa
100 MPa

Other units such as bar or atm may be given where historically or experimentally useful, preferably with an SI equivalent where this helps the reader.

Do not use depth as a direct synonym for hydrostatic pressure.

# 18. Water activity

Use the symbol:

(a_w)

Define water activity at first substantive use.

Do not treat water activity and water content as equivalent.

# 19. Thermodynamics

Use ΔG for Gibbs free-energy change under the actual conditions being discussed.

Use appropriate standard-state notation consistently, for example ΔG° or biochemical standard-state notation where applicable.

Environmental calculations should distinguish standard-state values from values calculated using actual temperature, pressure and reactant/product activities or concentrations.

State clearly whether thermodynamic quantities refer to:

* a reaction;
* a mole of substrate;
* a mole of electrons;
* another defined basis.

Energy units should normally be expressed as:

kJ mol⁻¹

Do not describe a thermodynamically favourable reaction as necessarily fast.

Thermodynamics and kinetics must remain conceptually distinct.

# 20. Redox terminology

Use **electron donor** and **electron acceptor** wherever this improves clarity.

Remember:

* the electron donor is oxidised;
* the electron acceptor is reduced;
* an oxidant accepts electrons;
* a reductant donates electrons.

Use redox potential terminology carefully and specify reference conditions when quantitative interpretation depends on them.

Do not describe an electron as intrinsically having “more energy” without explaining the redox context.

# 21. Metabolic terminology

Keep the following dimensions conceptually separate:

**Energy source**

* light;
* chemical reactions.

**Electron donor**

* inorganic;
* organic.

**Carbon source**

* inorganic carbon;
* organic carbon.

Thus, terms such as phototroph, chemotroph, lithotroph, organotroph, autotroph and heterotroph describe different aspects of metabolism and should not be treated as interchangeable.

Use compound terms when useful:

chemolithoautotroph
photoheterotroph
chemoorganoheterotroph

Do not equate chemolithotrophy with autotrophy. Many chemolithotrophs are autotrophic, but the terms describe different properties.

# 22. Extremophile terminology

Distinguish **-phile** from **-tolerant**.

A -phile has an optimum for growth or activity under the relevant condition.

A tolerant organism can withstand the condition while having its optimum elsewhere.

Where definitions depend on specific cardinal limits, provide the criteria being used and acknowledge that thresholds may vary among authors.

Avoid presenting operational categories as immutable biological boundaries.

# 23. Acronyms and abbreviations

Define abbreviations at first use unless they are universally familiar to the intended audience.

Example:

> extracellular polymeric substances (EPS)

Use EPS thereafter.

Do not introduce an abbreviation that appears only once or twice.

Standard chemical formulae such as DNA, RNA, ATP, CO₂ and H₂ do not normally require definition for the target audience, although the underlying concept may need explanation.

# 24. Italics and emphasis

Use italics for:

* genus and species names;
* bacterial and archaeal gene symbols;
* newly introduced technical terminology on first substantive use;
* occasional terms being explicitly discussed as terms.

Do not use italics merely for rhetorical emphasis.

Use **bold** only for rare, high-value conceptual anchors that express a memorable scientific principle. Do not bold ordinary factual sentences.

Do not use underlining for emphasis.

The pedagogical hierarchy should come primarily from good writing, headings, figures and boxes rather than typographic emphasis.

# 25. Numbers in prose

Spell out numbers from one to nine in ordinary prose when they are not attached to units or part of a technical expression.

Use numerals for:

* 10 and above;
* measurements;
* concentrations;
* temperatures;
* ages;
* percentages;
* numbered pathways or groups where numerals improve clarity.

Examples:

> Three major mechanisms have been proposed.

> Samples were incubated at 4 °C.

Do not apply this rule mechanically where it reduces clarity.

# 26. Dates and geological time

Use unambiguous date formats.

Prefer:

25 July 2026

over:

25/07/26

For geological time use accepted units and notation consistently:

ka
Ma
Ga

Explain these units on first use for readers outside Earth sciences.

Distinguish ages before present from durations where this matters.

# 27. Equations

Use equations only when they improve conceptual understanding.

Every important equation should be followed by an explanation of:

* what the variables represent;
* what changing them means physically or biologically;
* why the equation matters for the subject being discussed.

Do not include an equation merely because it is conventional.

Mathematical notation should use LaTeX-compatible Markdown.

# 28. Figures

Figures are explanatory tools, not decoration.

Prefer figures that show:

* mechanisms;
* gradients;
* causal relationships;
* spatial organisation;
* pathways;
* comparisons;
* environmental structure.

Common figure types include:

### Conceptual diagrams

Example: environmental stress → cellular consequence → adaptation.

### Process diagrams

Example: electron transport, carbon fixation or hydrothermal circulation.

### Environmental schematics

Example: geochemical gradients and microbial niches across a sediment profile.

### Data figures

Use only when actual quantitative data help explain an important concept.

### Photographs

Use when seeing the organism, structure, instrument or environment materially improves understanding.

Every figure must:

* be cited in the text;
* have a self-contained caption;
* identify the source or authorship;
* have clearly established reuse rights;
* remain interpretable in both digital and print versions.

Where possible, create original diagrams rather than reproducing published figures.

Prefer editable and versionable formats such as SVG.

Do not use copyrighted figures copied from publications or websites in the final book without appropriate permission or licence.

# 29. Figure captions

Captions should explain what the reader should notice, not merely repeat the title.

A good caption should normally allow the figure to be understood without rereading several paragraphs of the chapter.

Define symbols, colours and abbreviations where necessary.

# 30. Tables

Use tables primarily for comparison.

Good examples include:

* comparing adaptation strategies;
* comparing carbon fixation pathways;
* comparing environmental constraints;
* comparing analytical approaches.

Avoid enormous catalogues of taxa or measurements unless the dataset itself conveys an important conceptual point.

Tables should be comprehensible without requiring extensive interpretation from the surrounding prose.

Use SI units in column headings where appropriate.

# 31. Boxes and case studies

Boxes allow additional depth without interrupting the main conceptual narrative.

Recommended recurring categories are:

## Concept box

Introduces a piece of chemistry, physics, geology or molecular biology needed for deeper understanding.

## Case study

Examines one organism, environment or experiment in greater detail.

## Methods box

Explains how a particular observation is made or how evidence is generated.

## Frontier box

Introduces an unresolved question, controversy or active research area.

## Application box

Connects fundamental microbiology to technology or society.

Examples include:

* stable isotopes as fingerprints of microbial metabolism;
* *Deinococcus radiodurans* and radiation resistance;
* the historical search for the upper temperature limit of life;
* underground hydrogen storage microbiology;
* the Lost City hydrothermal field;
* microbial life in Antarctic subglacial environments.

Boxes should normally remain understandable independently of the main chapter.

# 32. Citations

The book is a textbook, not a review article.

Citations should support:

* quantitative claims;
* important discoveries;
* claims that may be controversial;
* specific limits of life;
* named mechanisms or landmark experiments;
* rapidly developing areas;
* direct attribution of ideas;
* data reproduced or adapted from published work.

Do not add citations mechanically to every sentence.

Conversely, do not make specific scientific claims without appropriate support merely because they originated in lecture material.

Prefer:

1. primary literature for discoveries and specific experimental claims;
2. authoritative reviews for broad synthesis;
3. established textbooks or reference works for general foundational material.

Avoid citing secondary websites when the primary scientific literature is available.

Never invent a citation.

If a suitable reference cannot be verified, mark the statement for reference checking rather than generating a plausible reference.

# 33. Reference management

References should ultimately be stored in the project's central BibTeX database.

Use stable, human-readable citation keys where practical.

Do not manually duplicate full bibliographic entries inside individual Markdown chapters.

DOIs should be included in bibliography records whenever available.

References must be checked for:

* correct authorship;
* title;
* year;
* journal/book;
* DOI;
* duplicate entries.

# 34. Further reading

Each substantial chapter should end with a curated **Further Reading** section.

This is distinct from the full bibliography.

Normally include approximately 5–10 carefully selected items representing, where appropriate:

* a foundational paper;
* one or more authoritative reviews;
* an accessible entry point for newcomers;
* methodological background;
* recent work illustrating current research directions.

Further Reading is editorial curation, not a list of every paper cited in the chapter.

A reader who wants to continue studying the subject should know where to start.

# 35. Historical material

History should be included when it helps explain:

* how a concept developed;
* why terminology exists;
* how limits or assumptions changed;
* how a discovery transformed the field.

Avoid chronologies containing names and dates that do not advance understanding.

Particularly valuable historical examples include changing estimates of the upper temperature limit of life and the development of chemolithotrophy.

# 36. Applications

Applications should grow naturally from the underlying biology.

Avoid sections that simply list potential commercial uses.

Explain why the relevant biological property produces the application.

For example:

thermostable enzymes → retention of function at high temperature → high-temperature industrial processes.

Applications should never dominate the fundamental scientific discussion unless the chapter itself concerns applications.

# 37. Astrobiology

Astrobiological implications should be scientifically constrained.

Clearly distinguish:

* observations on Earth;
* experimentally demonstrated limits;
* plausible extrapolation;
* planetary environmental constraints;
* speculation about extraterrestrial life.

Do not imply that identifying an Earth analogue demonstrates that life exists or could necessarily originate under the corresponding extraterrestrial conditions.

# 38. Taxonomy versus function

Taxonomic information is important but secondary to biological reasoning unless phylogeny itself is the subject.

Prefer:

**what organisms do → how they do it → why it matters → who does it**

rather than starting with long taxonomic lists.

Representative organisms should be selected because they illustrate a principle.

A representative organism should normally perform its main pedagogical work once. Later mentions should add genuinely new information or use a brief cross-reference rather than retelling the same experiment.

Extremophile chapters should normally include approximately three to six memorable representative organisms when suitable, distributed across distinct mechanisms, discoveries, ecological roles or limits.

Ecological significance should be concrete. Avoid generic claims that microorganisms are important; prefer a defined process, flux, reservoir, magnitude or ecological effect, using quantitative values only where robust evidence supports them.

# 39. Cross-chapter repetition

Foundational concepts should be taught properly once and subsequently cross-referenced.

Examples include:

* membrane architecture;
* Gibbs free energy;
* chemiosmosis;
* cardinal growth parameters;
* water activity;
* electron donors and acceptors;
* carbon fixation;
* osmotic stress;
* protein stability;
* sequencing terminology.

Later chapters may briefly remind the reader of a concept but should not independently recreate the complete explanation.

# 40. Cross-references

Use descriptive cross-references where practical.

Prefer:

> As discussed in Chapter 10, the energetic yield of a reaction depends strongly on environmental conditions.

over:

> As discussed previously…

Avoid cross-references that depend unnecessarily on chapter numbers if the publishing system can support stable labels.

Never refer the reader elsewhere before providing enough explanation to understand the current argument. Give the minimum sufficient explanation first, then cross-reference the chapter containing the full treatment.

# 41. Chapter openings

A chapter should normally begin by establishing why the subject matters.

Good openings may use:

* a conceptual problem;
* a striking observation;
* a short historical episode;
* an environmental contradiction;
* a well-chosen example.

Avoid generic introductions such as:

> Microbiology is a fascinating and rapidly evolving field.

The opening should lead directly into the intellectual problem of the chapter.

# 42. Chapter conclusions

Do not mechanically summarise every section.

A short concluding section is useful when it:

* integrates the central ideas;
* returns to the opening question;
* identifies what the reader should now be able to explain;
* points towards unresolved questions or the next conceptual chapter.

Avoid formulaic conclusions.

# 43. Accessibility

Accessibility means explaining difficult ideas clearly, not removing them.

When introducing a technically demanding concept:

* explain the problem first;
* introduce the formal terminology;
* use a figure when appropriate;
* give one concrete example;
* return to the general principle.

Avoid unexplained specialist jargon.

Avoid excessive parenthetical qualifications that make prose difficult to follow.

# 44. Markdown conventions

Use Markdown headings hierarchically.

```markdown
# NN. Chapter title

## Major section

### Subsection

#### Minor subsection
```

For publication chapters, the visible H1 must include the two-digit chapter number and title, consistent with front matter, filename identity and the canonical TOC.

Do not skip heading levels without reason.

Do not use headings solely for a single paragraph unless structurally necessary.

Use Markdown emphasis consistently:

```markdown
*italic*
**bold**
```

Scientific notation and equations should use the LaTeX syntax supported by the TeachBooks/Jupyter Book build.

Internal links and figure paths should be relative wherever practical to preserve portability.

# 45. File and asset naming

Use descriptive, lowercase file names with hyphens or underscores according to the repository convention eventually adopted.

Avoid spaces in file names where practical.

Examples:

```text
psychrophiles.md
hydrothermal-vent-schematic.svg
carbon-fixation-pathways.svg
```

Figures should have stable descriptive names rather than:

```text
figure1_final_new_v3.png
```

# 46. AI-assisted writing

AI tools may be used to:

* organise source material;
* identify duplication;
* improve clarity;
* restructure prose;
* generate alternative explanations;
* audit terminology;
* identify statements requiring references;
* draft figure specifications;
* check internal consistency.

AI tools must not be treated as scientific sources.

AI-generated references must never be accepted without verification.

AI-generated scientific claims must be checked against reliable literature when they extend beyond supplied source material.

AI must not silently expand the scope of a chapter merely to make it appear comprehensive.

Most importantly:

> Preserve the reasoning, examples, conceptual sequence and pedagogical intent of the source material. Do not preserve weak wording merely because it exists, and do not replace distinctive teaching logic with generic textbook prose.

# 47. Version philosophy

The book is intentionally a living resource.

A version does not need to cover every conceivable extreme environment or microbial strategy.

Version 1.0 should contain the strongest coherent material available at the time of publication.

Later versions may add:

* new environments;
* new case studies;
* improved figures;
* new methodological developments;
* revised classifications;
* new research frontiers.

Part IV, in particular, should remain open to contributions from recognised experts on specific environments.

Quality takes precedence over completeness.

# 48. Editorial priority

When rules conflict, use the following order of priority:

1. **Scientific accuracy**
2. **Correct representation of evidence and uncertainty**
3. **Conceptual clarity**
4. **Pedagogical usefulness**
5. **Consistency with the book's intellectual framework**
6. **Conciseness**
7. **Stylistic consistency**

No style rule should force scientifically misleading language.

# 49. Final author checklist

Before a chapter is considered ready for scientific review, confirm that:

* the chapter has a clear conceptual purpose;
* prerequisite concepts are either explained or appropriately cross-referenced;
* the narrative is causal rather than primarily descriptive;
* specialist terminology is defined on first substantive use;
* organism, gene and protein nomenclature follows the conventions above;
* units and chemical notation are consistent;
* quantitative claims are referenced;
